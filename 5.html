1 Why Word Vectors?

Core Problem: Document similarity using TF vectors works at the document level — but what if individual words mean similar things?
sim(watch, clock) > sim(watch, elephant) — but TF vectors can't tell us this.

The Distributional Hypothesis

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps." — J.R. Firth, 1957

Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. This is called the distributional hypothesis.

Why TF Vectors Fail for Word Similarity

Works: sim(A,B) > sim(A,C)
A: "I checked the time on my watch"
B: "I checked the time on my clock"
C: "I saw an elephant at the zoo"
Fails: sim(A,B) = sim(A,C)
A: "I checked the time on my watch"
B: "I checked the time on my clock"
C: "I checked the time on my elephant"

2 Distributional Word Vectors

Strategy: Represent Words by Context

Slide a context window around each occurrence of a word in a large corpus. Count how often each other word appears nearby.
…replace your smartphone and if the apple watch follows the pattern…
…each gallons of fill water with a watch time the amount of time it takes…
…you update on one device watch video anything you watch on one device…
watch = {video: 3168, apple: 1702, time: 868, …}
clock = {time: 2614, hour: 806, alarm: 438, …}
elephant = {baby: 108, species: 100, ears: 97, …}

Problem: Sparse Vectors are Huge

Vector sizes:
|watch| = 21,916 non-zero values
|clock| = 9,093 non-zero values
|elephant| = 5,477 non-zero values

These are memory and compute intensive. A vocabulary of 50,000 words means 50,000-dimensional vectors!

Solution: Truncated SVD — Dense Vectors

Apply Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to compress the sparse word-context matrix into dense low-dimensional vectors.
\[ \underbrace{M}_{V \times V} = \underbrace{U}_{V \times n} \cdot \underbrace{\Sigma}_{n \times n} \cdot \underbrace{V^T}_{n \times V} \]
M = original sparse co-occurrence matrix  |  U = dense word vectors (keep only n columns)  |  n ≈ 100–1000 dimensions

Cosine Similarity

\[ \text{sim}(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}||\vec{v}|} = \frac{\sum_i u_i v_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i u_i^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}} \]
Why cosine and not Euclidean? Cosine ignores vector magnitude — only cares about direction. Two words appearing in the same contexts (same proportions) get similarity = 1 regardless of how frequent they are.

Practice Questions — Cosine Similarity

Worked Calculation Practice

  1. [Exam-style] Compute cosine similarity between run = [4, 2, 0] and sprint = [3, 3, 1].
    Dot product = 4×3 + 2×3 + 0×1 = 12 + 6 + 0 = 18
    |run| = √(16 + 4 + 0) = √20 ≈ 4.472
    |sprint| = √(9 + 9 + 1) = √19 ≈ 4.359
    cosine = 18 / (4.472 × 4.359) = 18 / 19.489 ≈ 0.924
    High similarity — as expected for near-synonyms.
  2. [Exam-style] Given king = [1, 0, 3] and banana = [0, 2, 1], compute cosine similarity. What does the result tell you?
    Dot product = 0 + 0 + 3 = 3
    |king| = √(1+0+9) = √10 ≈ 3.162
    |banana| = √(0+4+1) = √5 ≈ 2.236
    cosine = 3 / (3.162 × 2.236) = 3 / 7.071 ≈ 0.424
    Low-to-medium similarity — these words are mostly unrelated, as expected.
  3. [Generated — harder] Why would two words with very different raw frequency counts still have cosine similarity ≈ 1.0?
    Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, not their magnitudes. If two words appear in the same types of contexts in the same proportions, their context vectors point in the same direction — even if one word is much more frequent (longer vector). Dividing by the product of magnitudes removes this length bias. Example: "happy" and "joyful" may have very different raw frequencies but similar directional vectors.
  4. [Exam-style — 2025 resit-inspired] Documents X and Y are represented as TF-IDF vectors. Doc X has non-zero entries for terms {A, C, G, H} and Doc Y for {A, B, D, E, G, H, I, J}. Calculate: (a) the overlap coefficient, (b) the Jaccard similarity.
    Intersection = {A, G, H} → |X ∩ Y| = 3
    |X| = 4, |Y| = 8, |X ∪ Y| = 9
    (a) Overlap = |X ∩ Y| / min(|X|, |Y|) = 3 / 4 = 0.75
    (b) Jaccard = |X ∩ Y| / |X ∪ Y| = 3 / 9 = 0.333

3 Static Word Embeddings: Pros & Cons

Advantage: Handles Synonymy

Words with similar/identical meanings will appear in similar contexts across the corpus, so they get similar embeddings.

sim(watch, clock) > sim(watch, elephant) ✓
Disadvantage: Fails on Polysemy

One word → one fixed vector. "bank" (river bank vs. financial bank) gets the same embedding regardless of which meaning is used.

vector("bank") = average of all meanings ✗
Key insight: The embedding for a polysemous word is a weighted average of all its meanings across the corpus. Less frequent meanings are underrepresented.

4 Contextual Word Vectors

Instead of vector(word), we want vector(word | context) — a different vector for each use of the word depending on its surrounding sentence.

Same word, different vectors based on context:

vector("match" | "I lit the _____")
fire meaning
vector("match" | "The _____ burned")
similar to above ✓
vector("match" | "They won the _____")
sports meaning — NOT similar ✗

ELMo: Early Contextual Vectors

ELMo (Embeddings from Language Models, 2018) used bidirectional LSTMs as a neural language model. The internal hidden states served as contextual embeddings, reusable for downstream tasks.
The key insight: A neural network trained to model language must internally learn something like meaning. Its internal representations are rich context vectors.

5 Self-Attention

Core idea: To build a context vector for word w, look at every other word in the sentence and ask: "How relevant is that word for understanding w?"

The Self-Attention Equation

\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V \]
where:
   \(Q = X W^Q\)   (queries — what am I looking for?)
   \(K = X W^K\)   (keys — what information do I provide?)
   \(V = X W^V\)   (values — what content do I pass forward?)
   \(d_k\) = dimension of queries/keys   (scaling prevents vanishing gradients)

Worked Example

Sentence: "The match burns brightly" — computing context vector for match:
WordRaw relevance scoreSoftmax weightRole
The12.10.00Filler — minimal weight
match91.10.86Self-relevance — highest
burns89.30.14Key context clue!
brightly44.70.00Less useful
\[ \text{softmax}([12.1,\; 91.1,\; 89.3,\; 44.7]) = [0.00,\; 0.86,\; 0.14,\; 0.00] \] \[ \text{context}(\textit{match}) = 0.00 \cdot v_\text{The} + 0.86 \cdot v_\text{match} + 0.14 \cdot v_\text{burns} + 0.00 \cdot v_\text{brightly} \]

Relevance Calculation Detail

\[ \text{relevance}(\text{"burns"} \mid \text{"match"}) = (x_\text{match} \, W^Q) \cdot (x_\text{burns} \, W^K)^T \]
Why not just cosine similarity? "match" and "burns" are not similar words — but "burns" is relevant to understanding "match". The learned WQ and WK matrices transform the vectors into a space where relevance (not similarity) is captured.

Softmax Function

\[ \text{softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \]
Converts any set of scores into a probability distribution summing to 1.

Why "Self"-Attention?

Called self-attention because the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence. It attends to itself. (Contrast with cross-attention where Q comes from one sequence and K,V from another — used in encoder-decoder translation.)
Where do WQ, WK, WV and the word vectors come from?

All of them are learned during training. The neural network adjusts these matrices through backpropagation to minimize the loss on the language modeling task (masked prediction or next-token prediction). There is no hand-crafting involved.

Why divide by √dk?

When dk is large, the dot products grow in magnitude, pushing softmax into regions with very small gradients (vanishing gradients problem). Dividing by √dk keeps the variance of the dot products at 1, making training more stable.

Practice Questions — Self-Attention

Softmax Calculation Drills

  1. [Exam-style] Raw attention scores for the sentence "The cat sat" when processing "cat" are [3, 7, 2]. Compute the softmax weights.
    \(e^3 = 20.09,\; e^7 = 1096.63,\; e^2 = 7.39\)
    Sum = 20.09 + 1096.63 + 7.39 = 1124.11
    Softmax = [20.09/1124.11, 1096.63/1124.11, 7.39/1124.11]
    = [0.018, 0.976, 0.007]
    Interpretation: "cat" attends almost entirely to itself (self-relevance score is dominant). "The" and "sat" contribute minimally.
  2. [Exam-style] Raw scores are [5, 5, 5] for three tokens. Compute the softmax. What does a uniform softmax mean?
    \(e^5 = 148.41\) for all three. Sum = 3 × 148.41 = 445.23
    Softmax = [148.41/445.23, 148.41/445.23, 148.41/445.23] = [0.333, 0.333, 0.333]
    A uniform distribution means the token attends equally to all positions — no token is considered more relevant than any other. This is the "no information" state.
  3. [Exam-style] For the sentence "bank collapsed yesterday" (3 tokens), the query vector for "bank" produces dot products with key vectors: The·K_bank = 9.5, q_bank·K_collapsed = 8.0, q_bank·K_yesterday = 3.0. With d_k = 64, compute the scaled scores and describe the result.
    √d_k = √64 = 8
    Scaled scores = [9.5/8, 8.0/8, 3.0/8] = [1.1875, 1.0, 0.375]
    Apply softmax: \(e^{1.1875}≈3.28,\; e^1≈2.72,\; e^{0.375}≈1.46\)
    Sum ≈ 7.46
    Weights ≈ [0.44, 0.36, 0.20]
    "bank" attends most to itself (likely financial meaning), then "collapsed" (strong contextual clue), then "yesterday".
  4. [Generated — conceptual] Why does the self-attention mechanism produce different context vectors for the word "bank" in "the river bank" vs "the bank collapsed"?
    The Q·K dot products produce different attention weights depending on the other tokens present. In "river bank", the word "river" has a high key-query match with "bank" → bank's context vector is heavily influenced by "river". In "bank collapsed", "collapsed" exerts high attention → the context vector shifts toward the financial meaning. The final context vector is a weighted sum of value vectors, so the surrounding words literally change what gets mixed in.

Conceptual Questions

  1. What is the difference between self-attention and cross-attention? Give one use case for each.
    Self-attention: Q, K, V all come from the same sequence. Each token attends to every other token in the same input. Used in BERT encoder layers to build contextual representations of a single sentence.
    Cross-attention: Q comes from one sequence (e.g. decoder state), K and V come from a different sequence (e.g. encoder output). Used in translation — the decoder queries the encoded source to decide which source words to focus on when generating each target word.
  2. How many learnable parameters are in a multi-head attention layer with h=8 heads, d_model=512? (Assume d_k = d_v = d_model/h = 64.)
    Each head has W_i^Q (512×64), W_i^K (512×64), W_i^V (512×64) = 3 × 32,768 = 98,304 params per head.
    8 heads × 98,304 = 786,432
    Plus output projection W^O (512×512) = 262,144
    Total ≈ 1,048,576 params (≈ 1M) just in the attention sublayer.

6 Subword Tokenization (BPE)

Problem — Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV) words: New words (neologisms, misspellings, domain-specific terms) not seen during training have no learned embeddings and zero probability.

Examples: staycation, deepfake, cryptocurrency, truthiness, microfinance
Solution — Subword tokenization: Split uncommon words into smaller pieces. Subwords appear more frequently, carry meaning clues, and reduce vocabulary size.

staycation → stay + ##cation

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) Algorithm

1
Pre-tokenize
Split corpus into words, removing whitespace
2
Init Vocab
All unique characters become initial symbols
3
Find Pair
Find most frequent adjacent symbol pair
4
Merge
Replace all instances of that pair with a new symbol
5
Repeat
Until desired vocabulary size reached

BPE Key Properties

Common words remain as single tokens
e.g. the, is, a
Rare words split into frequent subwords
e.g. running → run + ##ning
Domain matters: a tokenizer trained on English newspapers performs poorly on German or scientific text.
Vocabulary size is a hyperparameter — typically 30,000–50,000 for large models.
Other subword tokenization methods
  • WordPiece — Used by BERT; similar to BPE but merges based on likelihood rather than frequency.
  • Unigram LM — Used by SentencePiece; starts with a large vocabulary and prunes it.
  • SentencePiece — Language-independent; treats text as a raw character stream (no pre-tokenization needed).

  3. [Generated — exam-style] A colleague claims: "BPE tokenization produces the same result regardless of what language the corpus is written in." Is this correct? Explain why or why not with an example.
    Incorrect. BPE learns merges from frequency statistics in the training corpus. A tokenizer trained on English text will have merge rules optimised for English character patterns. When applied to German, Chinese, or other languages: German compound nouns (e.g. "Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft") would be split into many small, meaningless pieces. Chinese has no spaces between words and characters have different frequency patterns entirely. The resulting tokens would be suboptimal — frequent character n-grams in the target language would not have been merged. You should train a new tokenizer on each target language's corpus.
  4. [2024 Exam Q1d — 6 marks] Discuss the effects of setting the BPE vocabulary size to: (a) 100 tokens, (b) 100,000,000 tokens.
    (a) Vocabulary = 100: Very few merges have occurred. Almost all words are split into individual characters. Tokens carry almost no meaning (a single character "a" conveys little). Sequences become extremely long, making training inefficient. The model struggles to learn useful representations.

    (b) Vocabulary = 100,000,000: Every word (and many multi-word phrases) gets its own token — essentially word-level tokenization or worse. The system cannot generalise to new words (OOV problem returns). With so many tokens, each token is seen rarely during training → poor embeddings. Defeats the entire purpose of subword tokenization. Model also becomes enormous (embedding matrix = vocab_size × d_model).
  5. [Generated — vocab counting] A BPE tokenizer starts with 26 unique characters as its initial vocabulary. After 200 merge steps, what is the vocabulary size? How does this compare to a typical production tokenizer?
    Each merge step adds exactly 1 new symbol.
    Final vocab size = 26 + 200 = 226 symbols.

    This is far too small for production use. BERT uses ~30,000 tokens (WordPiece), GPT-2 uses ~50,257 BPE tokens, and LLaMA uses ~32,000 (SentencePiece). A vocabulary of 226 would result in almost every word being split into individual characters, making sequences very long and embeddings poor.

Conceptual BPE Questions

  1. [2024 Exam Q1b] A colleague says: "If we collect enough training data, we can eventually eliminate the need for an [UNK] token entirely." Do you agree? Justify your answer.
    Disagree. Unicode defines over 149,000 characters and new characters are added regularly. A fixed training corpus, no matter how large, cannot cover every possible Unicode character. Additionally, new characters, symbols, and emojis are continuously added to Unicode standards. The tokenizer vocabulary is fixed after training — it cannot dynamically expand. Therefore, [UNK] is always necessary to handle genuinely out-of-vocabulary input that has never been seen, no matter how comprehensive the training data.
  2. [Generated] The word "BeStep" (a brand name) gets tokenized by BERT as ["best", "##ep"]. This causes a sentiment classifier to incorrectly label negative reviews as positive. Explain why this happens and how you would fix it.
    Why it happens: BPE/WordPiece splits "BeStep" at a morpheme boundary that happens to coincide with the word "best". During pre-training, "best" appeared in strongly positive contexts, so its embedding carries positive sentiment signal. The classifier picks up on this spurious signal and assigns positive sentiment even to negative reviews about the brand.

    Fix: Replace the brand name "BeStep" with a neutral placeholder token like "BRAND" or "[BRAND]" in all text before passing to the tokenizer. This prevents the accidental sentiment leakage from the brand name's tokenization.

7 Transformers

Transformers combine self-attention, subword tokenization, positional encodings, and other techniques into a deep neural architecture. Proposed in "Attention is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017).

Problem: Attention Has No Positional Awareness

Self-attention treats input as a set — the order of words is irrelevant to the computation. But word order matters!

"Dog bites man" ≠ "Man bites dog"

Positional Encodings

Add a positional embedding vector to each token's word vector before attention. These can be:
  • Learned: treated as parameters, trained alongside the model
  • Sinusoidal: fixed using sin/cos functions at different frequencies
\[ PE_{(\text{pos}, 2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d_\text{model}}}\right), \quad PE_{(\text{pos}, 2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d_\text{model}}}\right) \]
Each position gets a unique vector; nearby positions have similar encodings.

Multi-Head Attention

Run self-attention multiple times in parallel, each with its own WQ, WK, WV matrices. Each "head" can specialize:
  • Head 1: grammatical relationships (subject-verb)
  • Head 2: coreference (which pronoun refers to what)
  • Head 3: semantic topic words
  • …BERT uses 12 heads; GPT-3 uses 96 heads
\[ \text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h) \, W^O \] \[ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(Q W^Q_i,\; K W^K_i,\; V W^V_i) \]

The Transformer Block

Input Embeddings + Positional Encodings
↓
Multi-Head Self-Attention
↓
Add & Norm (Residual)
↓
Feed-Forward Network
↓
Add & Norm (Residual)
↓
Output Context Vectors
Add (Residual connection): Output = LayerInput + LayerOutput. Provides a bypass path — easier gradient flow during training.
Norm (Layer Normalization): Keeps vector magnitudes stable so training doesn't diverge.
Feed-Forward Network: A standard 2-layer MLP applied to each position independently. Allows encoding non-linear, complex relationships beyond what attention captures.

Stacking Layers

Transformers stack N identical blocks. Each layer builds richer representations:
  • Lower layers: syntax (POS, basic grammar)
  • Middle layers: entity and co-reference information
  • Higher layers: semantic reasoning, task-specific features
BERT-base: 12 layers | BERT-large: 24 layers | GPT-3: 96 layers

Why Transformers Took Over

Parallelizable
All tokens processed simultaneously — unlike RNNs which process sequentially
Long-range context
Every token directly attends to every other token — no information bottleneck
Scalable
More data + more parameters = better performance. GPU-friendly computation

Training the Decoder (Teacher Forcing)

During training, we don't generate one word at a time. Instead, we use causal masking: token at position i is only allowed to attend to positions 0…i-1. This lets the entire sequence be processed in one forward pass while still training left-to-right prediction.

Practice Questions — Encoder/Decoder Architectures

Architecture Classification

  1. [Exam-style] For each task below, state the most appropriate transformer architecture (encoder-only, decoder-only, or encoder-decoder) and justify your choice:
    (a) Named Entity Recognition on medical reports
    (b) Automatic translation from English to French
    (c) Generating creative stories from a prompt
    (d) Classifying tweets as offensive or not
    (a) NER on medical reports → Encoder-only (e.g. BERT). NER is a token classification task — needs full bidirectional context to label each token. No generation required.

    (b) Translation → Encoder-Decoder (e.g. T5, original Transformer). Input and output are different sequences in different languages. The encoder processes the source, the decoder generates the target using cross-attention to the encoder output.

    (c) Story generation → Decoder-only (e.g. GPT-3, LLaMA). Text generation is the native task of decoder models, which produce tokens autoregressively conditioning on left context.

    (d) Tweet classification → Encoder-only (e.g. BERT, RoBERTa). Classification is an understanding task. Bidirectional context helps capture sarcasm and full-sentence meaning. [CLS] token vector fed to a classification head.
  2. [Generated — 2025 exam-style, 2 marks] Rank the following models by computational cost at inference time, from cheapest to most expensive: BERT-base, LLaMA-70B, Logistic Regression. Justify your ranking.
    Cheapest → Most expensive: Logistic Regression < BERT-base < LLaMA-70B

    Logistic Regression: A simple linear classifier. Once features are extracted (e.g. TF-IDF), inference is just a dot product + sigmoid — microseconds on CPU.
    BERT-base: 110M parameters, 12 transformer layers. Requires a full forward pass but is manageable on a single GPU. Seconds per batch.
    LLaMA-70B: 70 billion parameters. Requires multiple high-end GPUs just to load the model weights (~140 GB). Each generation step is expensive. Unsuitable for latency-sensitive applications without quantization.
  3. [Generated] BERT uses [MASK] tokens during pre-training. GPT-style models have no [MASK] token. Why? What implication does this have for using each model for text generation?
    BERT is trained with Masked Language Modeling (MLM): 15% of tokens are masked, and the model predicts them using both left and right context. The [MASK] token is a training device — it marks positions to predict. BERT does not generate text token-by-token; it fills in blanks. Generating open-ended text requires iterative masking tricks that are awkward and slow.

    GPT models predict the next token left-to-right (causal LM). No masking is needed — every token is a prediction target conditioned on its predecessors. This makes generation natural: just keep predicting the next token until an end token. But GPT cannot see future tokens, so it performs worse on tasks requiring full-sentence context (like NER or classification from a single token).

9 Pre-training & Fine-tuning

Paradigm: Train a large model on a language task with massive data (pre-training), then adapt it to a specific task with smaller labelled data (fine-tuning).

Practice Questions — Pre-training & Fine-tuning

Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2025 Resit-style — 4 marks] You want to build a sentiment classifier for social media posts with 4 sentiment classes. Describe the process for determining whether Logistic Regression, BERT, or LLaMA is most effective for this task.
    1. Collect labelled data: Gather social media posts labelled with the 4 sentiment classes.
    2. Split data: Divide into training, validation (dev), and test sets (e.g. 70/15/15).
    3. Train/tune each model: Train Logistic Regression on TF-IDF features; fine-tune BERT on the training set; prompt-tune or fine-tune LLaMA. Use validation set for hyperparameter selection.
    4. Evaluate on test set: Compare using Precision, Recall, and F1-score (macro-averaged across all 4 classes, since classes may be imbalanced).
    5. Choose the model with the highest macro-F1 on the held-out test set.
  2. [2024 Exam Q3d — 6 marks] A researcher fine-tunes BERT on indirect answer classification. They find that intermediate fine-tuning on MNLI (natural language inference) improves performance, but intermediate fine-tuning on BoolQ (boolean question answering) hurts performance. Explain why these intermediate tasks have opposite effects.
    Transfer learning motivation: Intermediate fine-tuning aims to transfer knowledge from a similar task before the final task.

    MNLI helps: Natural language inference requires understanding entailment, contradiction, and neutrality between statements. This is structurally similar to indirect answers — both require understanding whether a response supports, contradicts, or is neutral to a question. The relevant reasoning skills transfer positively.

    BoolQ hurts: BoolQ involves direct yes/no answers to questions. The model learns to look for explicit "yes/no" signals. Indirect answers deliberately avoid such direct signals — the entire challenge is that they express positions implicitly. Training on BoolQ pushes the model toward expecting directness, which is the opposite of what indirect answer classification requires. This knowledge is detrimental, not neutral.
  3. [Generated — 2025 exam-style, 4 marks] A BERT model fine-tuned on English sentiment analysis is deployed on Italian social media posts. A Logistic Regression baseline is also tested. Describe the specific failure modes of each approach on Italian text.
    Logistic Regression failure: The feature weights are tied to specific tokens observed in English training data. Italian uses almost entirely different tokens — most would be [UNK] or zero-weight. The classifier essentially sees nothing useful and defaults to majority class or noise predictions.

    BERT failure: bert-base-uncased's WordPiece tokenizer was trained on English text. Italian words would be split into character-level or nonsensical subword pieces that were never seen in context. The embeddings for these split tokens carry no meaningful Italian semantic signal. Even if individual subwords happen to exist in BERT's vocabulary, the model has never learned their Italian-language contextual relationships.

    Solution for both: Use a multilingual model (e.g. mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) trained on multiple languages, or collect Italian labelled data and train new models from scratch on it.
  4. [Generated] Explain what "freezing" a transformer encoder means during fine-tuning. When would you freeze vs when would you update all parameters? What is LoRA and why is it useful?
    Freezing: Setting encoder parameters as non-trainable. Only the newly added task-specific head learns. The pre-trained weights remain exactly as downloaded.

    Freeze when: Very little labelled data (avoids overfitting), limited compute budget, or when the task is very similar to pre-training and existing representations are already good.

    Full fine-tuning when: More labelled data available, task differs significantly from pre-training, or maximum performance is needed.

    LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation): Adds small low-rank matrices alongside frozen weight matrices. Instead of updating a full W (e.g. 768×768 = 589,824 params), LoRA adds W + BA where B is 768×r and A is r×768 with r≪768 (e.g. r=8). Only B and A are trained (~12,288 params vs 589,824). Reduces GPU memory by 10–100x while achieving performance close to full fine-tuning. Especially useful for LLMs with billions of parameters.

True / False

  1. Static word embeddings assign a different vector to each occurrence of "bank" depending on its context.
    FALSE. Static embeddings assign one fixed vector per word type regardless of context. This is precisely why contextual embeddings (BERT, ELMo) were developed.
  2. The distributional hypothesis states that words appearing in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.
    TRUE. Attributed to Firth (1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."
  3. In multi-head attention, all heads share the same WQ, WK, WV matrices.
    FALSE. Each head has its own independent WQi, WKi, WVi matrices, allowing different heads to capture different types of relationships.
  4. BPE tokenization requires a fixed hand-crafted vocabulary defined before training.
    FALSE. BPE learns its vocabulary (and the merge rules) from a corpus during a training phase. The only hyperparameter set in advance is the desired vocabulary size.
  5. During fine-tuning, it is always necessary to update all parameters of the pre-trained model.
    FALSE. You can freeze the pre-trained encoder and only train the new task-specific head. Full fine-tuning is an option but not a requirement.
  6. Transformers process tokens sequentially (one at a time), making them slower than RNNs on GPUs.
    FALSE. Transformers process all tokens in parallel via matrix operations, making them much faster than RNNs on modern GPU hardware — a key reason for their dominance.
  7. The softmax function in self-attention ensures that the attention weights across all tokens sum to 1.
    TRUE. Softmax converts raw scores into a probability distribution: all weights are non-negative and sum to 1, usable as weighted average coefficients.

Short Answer Questions

  1. Explain the difference between synonymy and polysemy and state which one static embeddings handle well and which they do not.
    Synonymy: two different words with similar meanings (e.g., watch/clock). Static embeddings handle this well because synonyms appear in similar contexts, so they get similar vectors. Polysemy: one word with multiple meanings (e.g., "bank" = financial institution or river bank). Static embeddings fail here because they give a single vector — a blurred average of all meanings — meaning less frequent senses are under-represented.
  2. What are Queries (Q), Keys (K), and Values (V) in self-attention? Give an intuition for each.
    Q (Query): what the current token is "looking for" — the question it asks of other tokens. K (Key): what each token "advertises" — the label attached to it for matching against queries. V (Value): the actual content a token contributes to the output once matched. The Q·KT dot product scores how well each key answers each query; the scores weight the values.
  3. Why do language models treat new words as OOV, and how does subword tokenization solve this?
    Word-level models have a fixed vocabulary. Any word not seen during training is unknown (OOV) with no learned embedding. Subword tokenization (e.g. BPE) splits rare words into smaller units that are likely to have been seen: "staycation" → "stay" + "cation". These subwords have embeddings and carry meaning, so the model can partially understand any new word.
  4. What is the purpose of residual (skip) connections in a Transformer block?
    Residual connections add the layer's input directly to its output: output = input + sublayer(input). This creates an alternative path for gradients to flow during backpropagation, avoiding the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks and making training much more stable and faster.
  5. Describe the pre-training and fine-tuning paradigm for BERT applied to sentiment analysis.
    1) Pre-train BERT: train on large unlabelled corpus with MLM and NSP objectives. 2) Remove head: strip the MLM prediction layer. 3) Add classification head: attach a small MLP that takes the [CLS] token's context vector and outputs sentiment labels (positive/negative). 4) Fine-tune: train on a labelled sentiment dataset, updating the head and optionally the encoder weights.

Long / Essay Questions

  1. Compare and contrast static word embeddings (e.g. Word2Vec) with contextual word embeddings (e.g. BERT). In your answer: (a) explain the distributional hypothesis, (b) describe how each type of embedding is constructed, (c) give a concrete example of a sentence where static embeddings fail but contextual embeddings succeed, and (d) discuss the computational trade-offs.
    A full answer should cover: (a) Distributional hypothesis — words in similar contexts have similar meanings (Firth 1957). (b) Static: sparse co-occurrence vectors → SVD or Word2Vec neural training → one fixed dense vector per word. Contextual: transformer with MLM/CLM training; each token gets a different vector depending on full sentence context, computed by stacking self-attention layers. (c) "I went to the bank" vs "The river bank flooded" — "bank" gets the same vector with Word2Vec but different context-dependent vectors with BERT. (d) Static embeddings are cheap to store and fast at inference; contextual embeddings require large model weights (hundreds of MB to GB) and forward pass computation at inference time.
  2. Explain the self-attention mechanism mathematically and intuitively. Why is the scaling factor 1/√dk necessary? What is multi-head attention and why is it beneficial?
    Intuition: each word "asks" what other words are relevant to understanding it. Math: Q=XWQ, K=XWK, V=XWV; scores = QKT/√dk; weights = softmax(scores); output = weights·V. Scaling: large dk causes large dot products → softmax saturates → near-zero gradients → training stalls. Dividing by √dk keeps variance at 1. Multi-head: run h parallel attention operations each with independent weight matrices; concatenate and project. Benefits: different heads capture different relationships (syntax, coreference, semantics) simultaneously; richer representations.
  3. Describe the Byte-Pair Encoding algorithm step by step. Starting from the corpus "peter piper picked a peck of pickled peppers", perform the first two merge steps manually.
    Algorithm: (1) Pre-tokenize into words; (2) Initialize vocab = all unique characters; (3) Find most frequent adjacent pair; (4) Merge all instances, add to vocab; (5) Repeat until target vocab size. Manual: Initial words = [[p,e,t,e,r],[p,i,p,e,r],[p,i,c,k,e,d],[a],[p,e,c,k],[o,f],[p,i,c,k,l,e,d],[p,e,p,p,e,r,s]]. Vocab = {a,c,d,e,f,i,k,l,o,p,r,s,t}. Step 1: most frequent pair = ('p','e') count=5 → merge to 'pe'. Step 2: most frequent pair = ('p','i') count=3 → merge to 'pi'. New vocab includes 'pe' and 'pi'.
  4. What is the encoder-decoder architecture and how does it enable machine translation? Explain the role of cross-attention in the decoder.
    The encoder processes the source sentence using bidirectional self-attention, producing context vectors for each source token. The decoder generates the target sequence token by token, using: (1) causal self-attention over already-generated target tokens; (2) cross-attention where Q comes from the decoder but K and V come from the encoder's outputs — this lets each generated word attend to the most relevant source words. Output sequence can differ in length from input. During training: teacher forcing with causal masking allows parallel computation. During inference: tokens generated autoregressively.

Calculation / Worked Examples

  1. Softmax calculation: Given raw attention scores [2, 4, 1] for tokens [The, match, burns] when processing "match", compute the softmax weights.
    \(e^2 = 7.39,\; e^4 = 54.60,\; e^1 = 2.72\)
    Sum = 64.71
    Softmax = [7.39/64.71, 54.60/64.71, 2.72/64.71] = [0.114, 0.844, 0.042]
    "match" attends mostly to itself (0.844) and slightly to "burns" (0.042).
  2. Cosine similarity: Compute cos(watch, clock) given:
    watch = [3, 0, 1], clock = [2, 1, 0]
    Dot product = 3×2 + 0×1 + 1×0 = 6
    |watch| = √(9+0+1) = √10 ≈ 3.162
    |clock| = √(4+1+0) = √5 ≈ 2.236
    cosine = 6 / (3.162 × 2.236) = 6 / 7.071 ≈ 0.849
  3. BPE vocabulary count: Starting vocabulary has 13 characters. After 4 merge steps, how large is the vocabulary?
    Each merge step adds exactly 1 new symbol to the vocabulary. After 4 merges: 13 + 4 = 17 symbols.
  4. Parameter counting: In a single-head attention layer with dmodel = 64, how many parameters are in WQ, WK, WV?
    Each matrix is dmodel × dk. If dk = dmodel = 64: each matrix has 64 × 64 = 4,096 parameters. Three matrices → 3 × 4,096 = 12,288 parameters.

2023 Exam — Questions Relevant to Lec 5

2023 — Q1: Tokenisation [20 marks]

  1. [Q1a — 2 marks] What is the difference between a word and a token? Why do NLP systems operate on tokens rather than words?
    Word: A sequence of letters representing an individual language unit (linguistic concept).
    Token: A sequence of characters that conveys meaning — may be a partial word (subword), punctuation, number, or whitespace.
    Systems operate on tokens because: (1) tokens are more granular units that can carry meaning individually; (2) non-word units like punctuation and numbers also convey meaning; (3) subword tokens handle OOV words and reduce vocabulary size.
  2. [Q1b — 2 marks] Why do tokenizers differ across languages? Give a concrete example.
    Conventions for what constitutes a meaningful unit differ across languages. A whitespace-based English tokenizer is ineffective on Chinese (no spaces between words) or Japanese (mixed scripts). German compound nouns (e.g. "Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft") need splitting — a single English word boundary rule would leave them unsplit. Tokenizers must be trained on each language's corpus to learn its specific meaningful unit boundaries.
  3. [Q1c — 8 marks] Apply BPE to the corpus "mississippi is pie". Show 4 merge steps with vocabulary size after each.
    Initial: m i s s i s s i p p i | i s | p i e   Vocab = {m,i,s,p,e} → size 5
    Step 1: Most freq pair = (i,s) ×3. Merge → "is". Vocab size: 6
    Corpus: m is s is s i p p i | is | p i e
    Step 2: Most freq pair = (is,s) ×2. Merge → "iss". Vocab size: 7
    Corpus: m iss iss i p p i | is | p i e
    Step 3: Most freq pair = (p,i) ×2. Merge → "pi". Vocab size: 8
    Corpus: m iss iss pi p i | is | pi e
    Step 4: Many pairs tied at 1. E.g. (m,iss)→"miss". Vocab size: 9
    Corpus: miss iss pi p i | is | pi e
  4. [Q1d — 4 marks] Give two advantages of BPE over rule-based tokenization, and two advantages of rule-based over BPE.
    BPE advantages over rule-based:
    (1) Data-driven: no fluent speaker required; works on any language with enough text.
    (2) Discovers useful patterns automatically; generalises to new domains with retraining.

    Rule-based advantages over BPE:
    (1) No training corpus required; rules developed with direct linguistic knowledge.
    (2) Interpretable and predictable; easy to debug specific tokenization decisions.
  5. [Q1e — 4 marks] A grammar suggestion assistant is being built. Should it use stemming, lemmatization, or stopword removal? Justify your answer.
    Should NOT use any of these.
    Stemming: Grammar errors often involve incorrect inflection (wrong word ending). Stemming strips these endings, making errors invisible — "runned" and "ran" become identical stems, so the error cannot be detected.
    Lemmatization: Treats inflectional variants as the same token, again hiding the grammatical error in the inflection.
    Stopword removal: Stopwords (the, is, a, of) are grammatically critical. "He go the store" vs "He goes to the store" — removing "the" and "to" hides the structural error. Grammar depends on exact function words.

2024 Exam — Questions Relevant to Lec 5

2024 — Q1: Tokenisation [20 marks]

  1. [Q1a — 4 marks] A colleague says stopwords should always be removed before passing text to a language model. Are they correct for (a) bag-of-words models and (b) transformer models? Explain with an example.
    (a) Bag-of-words: Correct to remove. BOW loses word order — word attachment is already unclear. Stopwords like "the", "on" add little discriminative signal and increase dimensionality. Example: "The cat sat on the mat" and "The mat sat on the cat" produce identical BOW vectors, so stopwords do not help distinguish meaning anyway.

    (b) Transformers: Incorrect to remove. Transformers are sequence models that use word order and context. Stopwords carry grammatical information. Example: "The cat sat on the mat" vs "The mat sat on the cat" — removing "on" loses the prepositional relationship; a transformer with the full sequence can distinguish subject/object roles via attention over all tokens including function words.
  2. [Q1b — 4 marks] A colleague says: "If we collect enough training data, we can eliminate the need for an [UNK] token entirely." Do you agree?
    Disagree. Unicode contains over 149,000 characters and continues to expand with new emoji, scripts, and symbols. No training corpus can include every future Unicode character. The tokenizer's vocabulary is frozen at training time — it cannot expand dynamically to accommodate new characters added after training. Therefore [UNK] is always necessary to handle genuinely unseen input characters.

2025 Resit Exam — Questions Relevant to Lec 5

2025 — Q3: Sentiment Analysis [20 marks]

  1. [Q3a — 2 marks] Rank by computational cost at inference time (cheapest to most expensive): Logistic Regression, BERT, LLaMA. Justify briefly.
    Logistic Regression < BERT < LLaMA
    Logistic Regression: lightweight linear classifier, dot product over feature vector — trivial computation.
    BERT: 110M parameter transformer, requires GPU forward pass — moderate cost.
    LLaMA: Multi-billion parameter decoder model — requires multiple GPUs just to hold weights, each generation step is expensive.
  2. [Q3b — 4 marks] Describe how you would determine which of Logistic Regression, BERT, and LLaMA is the most effective approach for classifying social media posts into 4 sentiment classes.
    1. Collect a labelled dataset of social media posts with 4 sentiment classes.
    2. Split into training, validation (dev), and test sets.
    3. Train/tune each method: LR on TF-IDF features; BERT fine-tuned on training set; LLaMA fine-tuned or prompted.
    4. Use validation set to tune hyperparameters.
    5. Evaluate all three on the held-out test set using Precision, Recall, and F1-score (macro-averaged).
    6. The method with highest macro-F1 on the test set is most effective.
  3. [Q3c — 4 marks] The system must be extended to support Italian. Describe a specific problem that would arise for (a) Logistic Regression and (b) BERT-base, and suggest a solution.
    (a) Logistic Regression: The feature weights are tied to specific English tokens. Italian tokens are almost entirely unseen → they map to zero-weight features. The classifier has no useful signal and predicts randomly or defaults to the majority class. Solution: re-train on Italian-labelled data with Italian TF-IDF features.

    (b) BERT-base: WordPiece vocabulary was trained on English. Italian words get split into meaningless English subword fragments — e.g. "gatto" (cat) might split as "ga + ##tto". These fragments have no meaningful Italian semantic embeddings. Solution: Use a multilingual model (mBERT or XLM-RoBERTa) trained on many languages including Italian, or collect Italian data and fine-tune on it.
  4. [Q3d — 4 marks] A brand "BeStep" is tokenized as ["best", "##ep"] by BERT's WordPiece tokenizer. This causes the sentiment classifier to misclassify negative reviews of BeStep products as positive. Explain the cause and propose a solution.
    Cause: WordPiece splits "BeStep" into "best" + "##ep". The sub-token "best" appears with positive sentiment in training data (e.g. "best movie", "best choice"). The BERT embedding for "best" therefore has a positive sentiment signal. When the model processes a negative review mentioning "BeStep", the "best" subtoken activates positive sentiment, overriding the negative content elsewhere in the review.

    Solution: Pre-process all text to replace the brand name "BeStep" with a neutral placeholder such as "BRAND" or "[PRODUCT]" before tokenization. This prevents the tokenizer from splitting the brand name into semantically misleading pieces, and a neutral token carries no spurious sentiment signal.
  5. [Q3e — 6 marks] The system needs to be extended to classify sentiment at the individual product level — 18 products, 4 sentiments each. Describe a classification scheme and evaluation strategy for this.
    Classification scheme: Model this as multi-label classification with output space = {product × sentiment} pairs. 18 products × 4 sentiments = 72 product-sentiment pairs, plus a "no product mentioned" class. For each post, the model can output multiple labels (e.g. positive about Product A, negative about Product C).

    Training data: Collect and annotate posts with per-product sentiment labels. This requires significantly more data than the original binary task.

    Evaluation: Use per-class Precision, Recall, F1 for each of the 72+ labels. Report macro-averaged F1 across all classes. Crucially, the test set must contain examples of all product-sentiment combinations to evaluate performance on rare pairings. Ensure the test set is not contaminated with near-duplicates of training examples.

  2. [Generated — 8 marks, 2023-exam-style] Explain the concept of polysemy and why it is a fundamental limitation of static word embeddings. Describe how transformers address this limitation through contextual representations. Use the word "crane" as a running example (meanings: the bird, the machine, to stretch one's neck).
    Polysemy: A single word form with multiple distinct meanings. "Crane" can be: (1) a tall wading bird, (2) a piece of construction equipment, (3) a verb meaning to stretch one's neck to see something.

    Static embedding failure: Word2Vec or GloVe assigns a single fixed vector to "crane". This vector is a weighted average across all meanings as they appear in the training corpus. If "crane" appears mostly in construction contexts, its vector will be biased towards machinery, and ornithology texts will give the wrong similarity scores. The three meanings are collapsed into one blurry representation.

    Transformer solution: BERT computes a different context vector for each occurrence of "crane" based on the entire surrounding sentence via self-attention. In "The crane swooped down to the river" — attention to "swooped" and "river" produces a bird-meaning vector. In "The crane lifted the steel beam" — attention to "lifted" and "steel beam" produces a machinery-meaning vector. In "She craned her neck to see" — verb usage produces a third distinct vector. The same token generates three distinct representations depending on context, resolving polysemy naturally.
  3. [Generated — 2024-style, dataset problems] A student builds a multilingual sentiment dataset for fine-tuning.     |v| = √(4+4+1) = √9 = 3
    cosine = 8 / (3×3) = 8/9 ≈ 0.889
  4. [Scaled attention] q_word·k_1 = 16, q_word·k_2 = 8, q_word·k_3 = 4. d_k = 16. Compute softmax over scaled scores.
    √16 = 4. Scaled = [16/4, 8/4, 4/4] = [4, 2, 1]
    e⁴ = 54.60, e² = 7.39, e¹ = 2.72
    Sum = 64.71
    Softmax = [54.60/64.71, 7.39/64.71, 2.72/64.71] = [0.844, 0.114, 0.042]
  5. [Multi-head attention parameters] h = 12 heads, d_model = 768, d_k = d_v = 64. How many parameters in W^O (output projection)?
    W^O projects from (h × d_v) back to d_model = (12 × 64) × 768 = 768 × 768 = 589,824 parameters. This is the BERT-base configuration.
  6. [Context vector dimensions] You pass a sentence with 12 tokens through BERT-base. What is the shape of the output tensor from the last hidden state?
    Shape = [batch_size, sequence_length, hidden_size] = [1, 12, 768]
    1 sequence, 12 tokens, each with a 768-dimensional context vector. Note: the actual input to BERT would have 12 + 2 = 14 tokens ([CLS] + 12 + [SEP]), giving shape [1, 14, 768].

Rapid-Fire True / False — Past Exam Topics

  1. In BERT, the [CLS] token is always placed at the end of the input sequence.
    FALSE. [CLS] is placed at the beginning of the sequence. [SEP] is placed at the end. Format: [CLS] tokens... [SEP].
  2. Beam search always produces a higher-probability output sequence than greedy decoding.
    TRUE. Beam search with k beams considers k candidates at each step, retaining the top-k partial sequences. Greedy is beam search with k=1. Therefore beam search explores more of the search space and cannot produce a lower-probability final sequence than greedy.
  3. A fine-tuned BERT model for English sentiment analysis can be directly applied to Chinese sentiment analysis without any modification.
    FALSE. BERT-base's WordPiece tokenizer was trained on English. Chinese characters would be tokenized as [UNK] or broken into meaningless fragments, producing useless embeddings. A multilingual model (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) or a Chinese-specific BERT is needed.
  4. Removing stopwords before fine-tuning a BERT sentiment classifier is good practice.
    FALSE. BERT is a sequence model that uses word order and context. Removing stopwords disrupts grammatical structure and can remove words that contribute to sentiment meaning. For example, "not bad" → "bad" after stopword removal completely reverses the sentiment. Leave stopwords in for transformer models.
  5. The vocabulary size of BPE is determined entirely by the frequency statistics in the training corpus, with no hyperparameter from the user.
    FALSE. The desired final vocabulary size is a hyperparameter set by the user (typically 30,000–50,000). BPE runs merge steps until this target size is reached. The merge rules themselves are determined by corpus statistics, but the stopping point is user-defined.
  6. A decoder-only model like GPT can be used for Named Entity Recognition by fine-tuning it on NER data.
    TRUE (with caveats). In principle yes — you can fine-tune a decoder model to output entity tags. In practice, encoder models like BERT perform better for NER because they have bidirectional context and are designed for per-token classification. GPT-style models see only left context at each token, which is suboptimal for NER where right-context clues matter.
