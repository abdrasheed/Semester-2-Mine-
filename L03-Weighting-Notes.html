Text Statistics & Word Frequency

Why Statistics Matter

Many statistical characteristics of word occurrences are predictable. IR models and ranking algorithms depend heavily on statistical properties of words to determine which terms best describe document content.

  • Important words occur often in a document but NOT in the whole collection
  • Very frequent words (stopwords) are poor discriminators
  • Very rare words (appear once) don't reliably describe content
  • Medium-frequency words tend to be the most descriptive

Zipf's Law

Zipf's Law — Rank-Frequency Relationship
\[ r \cdot f \approx k \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad r \cdot P_r \approx A \]

Where: r = rank of a word (by decreasing frequency), f = frequency, k is a constant, P_r = probability of occurrence, A ≈ 0.1 for English.

Practice Example

In a corpus, the most frequent word has rank r=1 and frequency f=1000. Using Zipf's Law, estimate the frequency of the word at rank r=5 and r=50.

Solved Example

From Zipf: r·f = k. For r=1, f=1000 → k = 1×1000 = 1000.
At rank r=5: f = k/r = 1000/5 = 200
At rank r=50: f = k/r = 1000/50 = 20
The 50th-ranked word occurs only 20 times vs 1000 for the top word — a very skewed distribution.

Zipf as a Power Law
\[ y = k x^c \quad (c = -1) \implies \log(y) = \log(k) - \log(x) \]

On a log-log plot, Zipf's Law gives a straight line with slope −1. The two most common English words ("the", "of") account for ~10% of all word occurrences.

Proportion of Words with Frequency n (Zipf)
\[ \text{Proportion}(\text{freq}=n) = \frac{1}{n(n+1)} \]
Practice Example

Using the Zipf proportion formula, calculate the proportion of words that appear exactly once (n=1) and exactly twice (n=2) in a large corpus.

Solved Example

For n=1: proportion = 1/(1×2) = 1/2 = 0.5 (50%)
For n=2: proportion = 1/(2×3) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 (16.7%)
Over 50% of vocabulary words appear only once — these "hapax legomena" are unhelpful for retrieval as they carry no generalisable signal.

Consequences of Zipf's Law for IR

High-frequency words (stopwords):

  • Few in number, very frequent
  • e.g., "the", "of", "and", "to"
  • Poor discriminators → remove
  • Corresponds to "closed-class" words in linguistics

Hapax legomena (appear once):

  • Many in number, very rare
  • Typos, proper names, invented words
  • Don't describe content reliably
  • May be kept in index but weighted low

Medium-frequency words are most useful!

The "resolving power" of a term peaks at medium frequencies — informative enough to describe content, but specific enough to discriminate between documents (Luhn 1957).

Heaps' Law — Vocabulary Growth

Heaps' Law
\[ V = K \cdot n^\beta \quad \text{with typically} \; K \approx 10\text{–}100, \; \beta \approx 0.5 \]

V = vocabulary size (unique terms), n = total number of words in corpus.

Practice Example

A corpus has 1,000,000 words. Using K=30 and β=0.5, estimate the vocabulary size V. If the corpus doubles to 2,000,000 words, how does V change?

Solved Example

n = 1,000,000: V = 30 × (1,000,000)^0.5 = 30 × 1000 = 30,000 unique terms
n = 2,000,000: V = 30 × (2,000,000)^0.5 = 30 × 1414 ≈ 42,400 unique terms
Vocabulary grows as a square root of corpus size — sub-linear growth. Vocabulary is not upper-bounded due to new words, typos, and proper names.

Term Weighting: TF and IDF

Motivation: Not All Terms Are Equal

Binary weights (present/absent) ignore how much a document talks about a term, and how specific a term is. Better approach: weight terms by (a) how often they appear in a document, and (b) how rare they are in the collection.

Term Frequency (TF)
\[ tf_{kd} = f_{kd} = \text{number of occurrences of term } k \text{ in document } d \]

If term k appears often in document d, then document d is likely to be "about" term k.

Practice Example

Document d = "the cat sat on the mat. the cat likes the mat." Compute tf(cat, d), tf(the, d), tf(dog, d).

Solved Example

tf(cat, d) = 2 (cat appears twice)
tf(the, d) = 4 (the appears four times)
tf(dog, d) = 0 (dog does not appear)
Note: "the" has higher tf than "cat" but is a stopword — less discriminative.

Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) — Sparck Jones, 1972
\[ \text{idf}(k) = \log \frac{N}{D_k} \]

N = total documents in collection, D_k = number of documents containing term k. Terms rare in the collection get high IDF.

Practice Example

A collection has N=1000 documents. Term "cat" appears in D_k=100 documents. Term "quantum" appears in D_k=5 documents. Compute IDF for each.

Solved Example

IDF(cat) = log(1000/100) = log(10) = 1.0 (base 10) or 2.303 (natural log)
IDF(quantum) = log(1000/5) = log(200) = 2.301 (base 10) or 5.298 (natural log)
"quantum" has much higher IDF — more specific and discriminative than "cat".

TF-IDF Weighting Variants
\[ w_{kd} = f_{kd} \cdot \log \frac{N}{D_k} \qquad \text{(basic)} \]
\[ w_{kd} = f_{kd} \cdot \log \frac{N - D_k + 0.5}{D_k + 0.5} \qquad \text{(probabilistic IDF)} \]
\[ w_{kd} = f_{kd} \cdot \log \left(\frac{N}{D_k} + 1\right) \qquad \text{(smoothed, handles } D_k = 0\text{)} \]
\[ W_{kd} = (1 + \log(f_{kd})) \cdot \text{IDF}_k \qquad \text{(sublinear TF)} \]

Sublinear TF moderates extreme values — prevents very high tf from dominating (useful against web spam).

Practice Example

Given: N=10000, tf(quantum, d)=5, D_k(quantum)=50. Compute w using (1) basic TF-IDF and (2) sublinear TF-IDF. Use log base 10.

Solved Example

Step 1 — IDF: log(10000/50) = log(200) = 2.301
Basic TF-IDF: w = tf × IDF = 5 × 2.301 = 11.505
Sublinear TF-IDF: (1 + log₁₀(5)) × 2.301 = (1 + 0.699) × 2.301 = 1.699 × 2.301 = 3.909
Sublinear gives much lower weight — a document with tf=5 vs tf=50 is not 10x more relevant, just somewhat more.

Python: TF-IDF Computation

import math
from collections import Counter

def compute_tf(doc_tokens):
    # Raw term frequencies
    return Counter(doc_tokens)

def compute_idf(docs, vocab):
    N = len(docs)
    idf = {}
    for term in vocab:
        Dk = sum(1 for d in docs if term in d)
        idf[term] = math.log(N / (Dk + 1))  # +1 to avoid div by 0
    return idf

def tfidf(tf, idf, term):
    return tf.get(term, 0) * idf.get(term, 0)

# Example
docs = [['cat','sat','mat','cat'],
        ['dog','sat','log'],
        ['cat','log','mat']]
vocab = {'cat','sat','mat','dog','log'}
idf = compute_idf(docs, vocab)
tf0 = compute_tf(docs[0])
print(f"TF-IDF(cat, doc0) = {tfidf(tf0, idf, 'cat'):.3f}")
print(f"IDF(cat)={idf['cat']:.3f}, IDF(dog)={idf['dog']:.3f}")

Robertson & Sparck Jones (RSJ) Probabilistic Framework

Motivation: Probabilistic Term Weighting

Robertson and Sparck Jones (1976) asked: what is the theoretically optimal IDF? They approached it from a probabilistic perspective: estimate the probability that a document is relevant given the presence/absence of a term.

  • p = P(term present | document is relevant)
  • q = P(term present | document is non-relevant)
  • If p > q, the term is a useful indicator of relevance
RSJ Term Weight (Probabilistic IDF)
\[ w_k = \log \frac{p_k(1 - q_k)}{q_k(1 - p_k)} \]

This is the log-odds ratio of relevant vs non-relevant. When relevance information is unavailable, assume all documents are equally likely to be relevant and simplify to the classic IDF.

Simplified RSJ (no relevance feedback)
\[ w_k \approx \log \frac{N - D_k + 0.5}{D_k + 0.5} \]

The 0.5 smoothing constants (Laplace correction) prevent zero weights when D_k = 0 or D_k = N. This is the probabilistic IDF used in BM25.

Robertson-Sparck Jones TF Component

Robertson also formalised the TF component. The key insight is that raw TF is problematic:

  • A document mentioning "cat" 10 times is not 10× more likely to be relevant than one mentioning it once
  • TF should be saturating — increasing returns diminish as tf grows
  • Long documents should be penalised for having high TF just by chance

Two key problems with raw TF:

1. TF grows without bound — term repetition yields unbounded weight.
2. Document length bias — long docs have higher TF just because they are longer, not because they are more about the topic.

TF Saturation & Document Length Normalisation

Saturating TF — Robertson's BM (Best Match) TF Component
\[ \text{tf\_sat}(k, d) = \frac{(k_1 + 1) \cdot f_{kd}}{k_1 + f_{kd}} \]

With parameter k₁ (typically 1.2–2.0). As fkd → ∞, this approaches (k₁+1). The function is concave — it saturates. Compare with raw TF: linear, no ceiling.

TF Saturation vs Raw TF

Saturating TF asymptotes at k₁+1. Raw TF grows linearly and gives unfair advantage to documents that spam terms.

Document Length Normalisation
\[ \text{normalised TF} = \frac{f_{kd}}{0.5 + 1.5 \cdot \frac{dl}{avgdl}} \]

Where dl = document length (number of tokens), avgdl = average document length in the collection. Documents longer than average are penalised; shorter ones are boosted. Parameter b = 0.75 controls the degree of length normalisation.

Why Length Normalise?

Verbosity problem:

A long document may have a high tf simply because it uses more words overall — not because it is "more about" the topic. E.g., a 10,000-word essay about cats vs a 200-word cat fact-sheet: the essay naturally has tf=50 but the fact-sheet is arguably more focused.

Scope problem:

Some documents are genuinely longer because they cover many topics. Length normalisation should not over-penalise truly comprehensive documents. Parameter b tunes this: b=0 means no length normalisation; b=1 is full normalisation.

Pivoted Document Length Normalisation (Singhal, 1996)

Observation: Retrieval Systems Systematically Disfavour Long Documents

Singhal et al. (1996) showed empirically that standard cosine normalisation by document length causes short documents to be over-ranked and long documents to be under-ranked relative to relevance. The "pivot" is the document length at which normalisation is correct.

Pivoted Length Normalisation
\[ \text{norm}(d) = (1 - b) + b \cdot \frac{dl}{\text{avgdl}} \]
\[ \text{tf}^{\text{pivoted}}_{kd} = \frac{1 + \ln(1 + \ln(f_{kd}))}{\text{norm}(d)} \]

Parameter b (0 to 1, typically 0.2 for SMART collections) controls the pivoting strength. When dl = avgdl, norm(d) = 1 and no change occurs — this is the pivot point.

Pivoted Normalisation Key Insight:

The "unique term" normalisation (dividing by number of unique terms instead of total length) correlates better with relevance probability. The pivot point is where the two normalisation curves cross — chosen so that average normalisation = 1.

BM25 (Okapi Best Match 25)

History and Motivation

BM25 was developed by Robertson, Walker, Jones, Hancock-Beaulieu, and Gatford at City University London for the TREC conferences in the 1990s (initially "Okapi" system, then formalised as BM25). It combines:

  • Saturating TF (prevents term-spamming)
  • Document length normalisation (pivoted around avgdl)
  • Probabilistic IDF (Robertson & Sparck Jones)
  • Query TF (separate saturation for query term frequency)

BM25 is the standard baseline in IR — highly competitive even against modern neural methods.

BM25 Full Formula
\[ \text{BM25}(d, q) = \sum_{k \in q} \underbrace{\log \frac{N - D_k + 0.5}{D_k + 0.5}}_{\text{IDF}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{(k_1 + 1) \cdot f_{kd}}{k_1 \left[(1 - b) + b \cdot \frac{dl}{avgdl}\right] + f_{kd}}}_{\text{TF component}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{(k_2 + 1) \cdot f_{kq}}{k_2 + f_{kq}}}_{\text{query TF}} \]

Where: fkd = term frequency in document, fkq = term frequency in query, dl = document length, avgdl = average document length, k₁ = TF saturation parameter (typically 1.2 or 2.0), b = length normalisation parameter (typically 0.75), k₂ = query TF saturation (typically 0 or 1000, often ignored for short queries).

BM25 Simplified (short queries, k₂ ignored)
\[ \text{BM25}(d, q) = \sum_{k \in q} \log \frac{N - D_k + 0.5}{D_k + 0.5} \cdot \frac{(k_1 + 1) \cdot f_{kd}}{k_1 \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{dl}{\text{avgdl}}\right) + f_{kd}} \]

Most implementations use this simplified form with default parameters k₁ = 1.2, b = 0.75.

BM25 Parameters

ParameterTypical ValueRoleEffect of increasing
k₁1.2 or 2.0TF saturationSlower saturation — more weight on high tf
b0.75Length normalisationStronger length penalty
k₂0Query TF saturationMore weight on repeated query terms

Key parameter behaviours:

b=0: No length normalisation (treat all docs equally regardless of length).
b=1: Full length normalisation (divide by dl/avgdl completely).
k₁=0: TF completely ignored — only presence/absence matters (Boolean-like).
k₁→∞: TF enters linearly — no saturation (like raw TF-IDF).

Worked BM25 Calculation — Full Example
Setup:

Collection: N=10,000 documents, avgdl=200 tokens.
Query: q = "machine learning"
Document d: dl=100 tokens, contains "machine" fmachine,d=4 times, "learning" flearning,d=3 times.
Dmachine=500, Dlearning=200.
Parameters: k₁=1.2, b=0.75.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1 — IDF for each term:
IDF(machine) = log((10000 - 500 + 0.5)/(500 + 0.5)) = log(9500.5/500.5) = log(18.98) ≈ 2.944
IDF(learning) = log((10000 - 200 + 0.5)/(200 + 0.5)) = log(9800.5/200.5) = log(48.88) ≈ 3.889

Step 2 — Length normalisation factor:
(1 - b) + b × (dl/avgdl) = (1 - 0.75) + 0.75 × (100/200) = 0.25 + 0.75 × 0.5 = 0.25 + 0.375 = 0.625

Step 3 — TF component for "machine" (f=4):
TF(machine) = (k₁+1) × f / (k₁ × norm + f) = (1.2+1) × 4 / (1.2 × 0.625 + 4) = 2.2 × 4 / (0.75 + 4) = 8.8 / 4.75 ≈ 1.853

Step 4 — TF component for "learning" (f=3):
TF(learning) = 2.2 × 3 / (1.2 × 0.625 + 3) = 6.6 / (0.75 + 3) = 6.6 / 3.75 ≈ 1.760

Step 5 — BM25 score:
BM25(d,q) = IDF(machine) × TF(machine) + IDF(learning) × TF(learning)
= 2.944 × 1.853 + 3.889 × 1.760
= 5.457 + 6.844 = 12.301

Second BM25 Worked Example — Compare Two Documents
Setup:

N=5000, avgdl=300. Query: "neural network". k₁=1.2, b=0.75.
Dneural=100, Dnetwork=250.
Doc A: dl=150, fneural=2, fnetwork=3.
Doc B: dl=600, fneural=4, fnetwork=6.

Solution:

IDF:
IDF(neural) = log((5000-100+0.5)/(100+0.5)) = log(4900.5/100.5) = log(48.76) ≈ 3.887
IDF(network) = log((5000-250+0.5)/(250+0.5)) = log(4750.5/250.5) = log(18.96) ≈ 2.942

Doc A (dl=150, norm = 0.25+0.75×150/300 = 0.25+0.375 = 0.625):
TF(neural) = 2.2×2/(1.2×0.625+2) = 4.4/2.75 ≈ 1.600
TF(network) = 2.2×3/(1.2×0.625+3) = 6.6/3.75 ≈ 1.760
Score_A = 3.887×1.600 + 2.942×1.760 = 6.219 + 5.178 = 11.397

Doc B (dl=600, norm = 0.25+0.75×600/300 = 0.25+1.5 = 1.75):
TF(neural) = 2.2×4/(1.2×1.75+4) = 8.8/(2.1+4) = 8.8/6.1 ≈ 1.443
TF(network) = 2.2×6/(1.2×1.75+6) = 13.2/(2.1+6) = 13.2/8.1 ≈ 1.630
Score_B = 3.887×1.443 + 2.942×1.630 = 5.608 + 4.796 = 10.404

Result: Doc A (shorter, more focused) scores higher despite Doc B having twice the term counts.
This is length normalisation at work — Doc B's higher tf is penalised because it is twice as long.

BM25+ — Lower Bound Guarantee

Problem with BM25: Zero Lower Bound on TF Component

In BM25, when fkd=0 (term absent from document), the TF component is 0. But when the document is very long, the length-normalised denominator grows large, and even a single occurrence of a rare term contributes very little score. This creates a counterintuitive situation: a document that mentions a query term once could score lower than a document that doesn't mention it at all — after IDF weighting when compared across documents of different lengths.

BM25 violation of term-matching guarantee:

Lv & Zhai (2011) showed that BM25 has no lower bound on the TF contribution — specifically, for long documents, adding a single occurrence of a query term can decrease the score relative to similar documents. This violates a desirable monotonicity property.

BM25+ Formula — Adding a Lower Bound δ
\[ \text{BM25+}(d, q) = \sum_{k \in q} \text{IDF}(k) \cdot \left[ \frac{(k_1 + 1) \cdot f_{kd}}{k_1 \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{dl}{\text{avgdl}}\right) + f_{kd}} + \delta \right] \]

The added constant δ (typically δ = 1) ensures that every document matching a query term gets a minimum positive boost. This guarantees the term-matching lower bound: any document containing a query term scores higher than one that doesn't.

BM25+ Worked Example

Using the same setup as BM25 example (N=10000, avgdl=200, k₁=1.2, b=0.75, δ=1):
Doc d: dl=100, fmachine=4, flearning=3.
IDF(machine)=2.944, IDF(learning)=3.889, norm=0.625.

(vs BM25 = 12.301; the δ adds a constant boost per matching query term)

BM25F — Field Weighting

Motivation: Documents Have Structure

Real documents have structured fields: title, body, URL, anchor text, abstract, etc. Terms appearing in the title are more salient than terms buried in the body. BM25F (Robertson et al., 2004) extends BM25 to handle field-based weighting.

Why not simply concatenate fields?

Naively concatenating all fields into one document ignores the different importance of fields and distorts document length normalisation (title text would be inflated by body text length).

BM25F: Pseudo-Term Frequency Across Fields
\[ \tilde{f}_{kd} = \sum_{s \in \text{fields}} w_s \cdot \frac{f_{kds}}{(1 - b_s) + b_s \cdot \frac{dl_s}{\text{avgdl}_s}} \]

Where ws = weight/importance of field s (e.g., wtitle=10, wbody=1), fkds = frequency of term k in field s of document d, dls = length of field s, bs = per-field length normalisation parameter.

BM25F: Full Scoring Formula
\[ \text{BM25F}(d, q) = \sum_{k \in q} \text{IDF}(k) \cdot \frac{(k_1 + 1) \cdot \tilde{f}_{kd}}{k_1 + \tilde{f}_{kd}} \]

After computing the pseudo-TF, the saturating TF formula is applied as in BM25. Note that length normalisation is done per field before combining, not on the whole document.

BM25F Worked Example

Query term k = "neural". Document d has 2 fields:
Title (s=1): f=1, dl=10, avgdl=8, b₁=0.5, w₁=5
Body (s=2): f=3, dl=200, avgdl=300, b₂=0.75, w₂=1

Field-normalised frequencies:
Title: 1 / ((1-0.5) + 0.5×10/8) = 1 / (0.5 + 0.625) = 1/1.125 ≈ 0.889
Body: 3 / ((1-0.75) + 0.75×200/300) = 3 / (0.25 + 0.5) = 3/0.75 = 4.000

Pseudo-TF: ˜f = 5×0.889 + 1×4.000 = 4.445 + 4.000 = 8.445

Saturating: (k₁+1)×˜f / (k₁ + ˜f) = 2.2×8.445/(1.2+8.445) = 18.579/9.645 ≈ 1.926
Then multiply by IDF(neural) to get the term's contribution to BM25F score.

Summary

Zipf's Law

r·f ≈ k. A power law: few very frequent words, many rare ones. Medium-frequency terms are most discriminative.

Heaps' Law

V = K·n^β. Vocabulary grows sub-linearly with corpus size. Index will grow but more slowly than corpus.

IDF (Sparck Jones 1972)

idf(k) = log(N/D_k). Terms rare in the collection get high weight — they discriminate documents. RSJ probabilistic IDF: log((N-D_k+0.5)/(D_k+0.5)).

TF-IDF

w = tf × idf. High weight = term appears often in this document AND is rare in the collection. Sublinear TF: (1+log(tf))×idf.

TF Saturation

BM25 TF component: (k₁+1)×tf / (k₁×norm+tf). Prevents term-spamming. Saturates at k₁+1. Parameter k₁=1.2–2.0.

Pivoted Normalisation

Singhal (1996): norm = (1-b)+b×dl/avgdl. Corrects for systematic under-ranking of long documents. b≈0.2 for cosine-based models.

BM25

State-of-the-art BM-family formula. Combines: probabilistic IDF × saturating TF × length normalisation. k₁=1.2, b=0.75 standard. Strong baseline.

BM25+

Adds lower-bound δ to TF component. Ensures term-matching is always non-negative. Fixes monotonicity violation in BM25 for long documents.

BM25F

Extends BM25 to structured fields (title, body, URL). Computes per-field normalised pseudo-TF, then applies standard BM25 saturation. Per-field weights ws and bs.

TRUE. When b=0, the length normalisation factor becomes (1-0)+0×(dl/avgdl) = 1, so it has no effect. All documents treated equally regardless of length.
T3. In BM25F, each field can have its own b parameter for length normalisation.
TRUE. BM25F has per-field parameters: weight w_s and length normalisation parameter b_s. For example, b_title=0.3 and b_body=0.75.
T4. The TF saturation ceiling in BM25 is k₁+1.
TRUE. As f_kd → ∞: (k₁+1)·f / (k₁·norm + f) → (k₁+1)·f/f = k₁+1. So k₁+1 is the asymptotic ceiling.
T5. BM25+ guarantees that matching a query term always results in a higher score than not matching it.
TRUE. The δ lower bound ensures the TF contribution is always ≥ δ > 0 for any matching term, regardless of document length. This is the term-matching guarantee BM25 lacks.
T6. Hapax legomena (words appearing once) are always removed from the inverted index.
FALSE. They may be kept in the index. They get near-zero TF-IDF weights due to IDF, so they rarely affect ranking, but are not necessarily discarded. Some systems do remove them to save space.
T7. Zipf's Law implies that about 50% of unique vocabulary items in a large corpus appear only once.
TRUE. Using the Zipf proportion formula for n=1: 1/(1×2)=0.5. Half the vocabulary is hapax legomena.
Calculation Problems
Problem 1. Collection: N=5000 docs. Query term "neural": D_k=50. Term "the": D_k=4990. Compute IDF for both. Which is a better discriminator?
IDF(neural) = log(5000/50) = log(100) = 2.0 (base 10)
IDF(the) = log(5000/4990) ≈ log(1.002) ≈ 0.00087
Neural is a far better discriminator — high IDF because it appears in only 1% of documents, while "the" appears in virtually all documents.
Problem 2. Doc d has term "algorithm" appearing tf=8 times. N=10000, D_k(algorithm)=200. Compute: (a) basic TF-IDF, (b) sublinear TF-IDF (log base 10).
IDF: log₁₀(10000/200) = log₁₀(50) = 1.699
(a) Basic TF-IDF: w = 8 × 1.699 = 13.592
(b) Sublinear TF: (1 + log₁₀(8)) × 1.699 = (1 + 0.903) × 1.699 = 1.903 × 1.699 = 3.233
Sublinear TF prevents documents that repeat a word 8 times from getting 8× the weight of documents that mention it once.
Problem 3. Zipf's Law: The 10th-ranked word has frequency 500. Estimate the frequency at rank 100 and rank 1000.
From Zipf: r·f = k. At r=10, f=500 → k = 10×500 = 5000.
At rank r=100: f = 5000/100 = 50
At rank r=1000: f = 5000/1000 = 5
This illustrates the heavy tail of language distributions.
Problem 4 (BM25). N=1000, avgdl=150. Query: "deep learning". D_deep=40, D_learning=80. Doc d: dl=100, f_deep=3, f_learning=2. Use k₁=1.2, b=0.75. Compute BM25(d,q).
IDF(deep): log((1000-40+0.5)/(40+0.5)) = log(960.5/40.5) = log(23.72) ≈ 3.166
IDF(learning): log((1000-80+0.5)/(80+0.5)) = log(920.5/80.5) = log(11.44) ≈ 2.437
Length norm: (1-0.75)+0.75×(100/150) = 0.25+0.5 = 0.75
TF(deep, f=3): (1.2+1)×3/(1.2×0.75+3) = 6.6/(0.9+3) = 6.6/3.9 ≈ 1.692
TF(learning, f=2): 2.2×2/(1.2×0.75+2) = 4.4/(0.9+2) = 4.4/2.9 ≈ 1.517
BM25(d,q): 3.166×1.692 + 2.437×1.517 = 5.357 + 3.697 = 9.054
Problem 5 (BM25F). Term "quantum": f in title=1 (dl_title=8, avgdl_title=7, b_title=0.4, w_title=8), f in body=2 (dl_body=300, avgdl_body=250, b_body=0.75, w_body=1). Compute the BM25F pseudo-TF, then the saturating TF with k₁=1.2.
Title field norm: (1-0.4)+0.4×(8/7) = 0.6+0.457 = 1.057
Title normalised f: 1/1.057 = 0.946
Title weighted: 8×0.946 = 7.568
Body field norm: (1-0.75)+0.75×(300/250) = 0.25+0.9 = 1.15
Body normalised f: 2/1.15 = 1.739
Body weighted: 1×1.739 = 1.739
Pseudo-TF: ˜f = 7.568 + 1.739 = 9.307
Saturating TF: (1.2+1)×9.307/(1.2+9.307) = 2.2×9.307/10.507 = 20.475/10.507 ≈ 1.949
Multiply by IDF("quantum") to get the term contribution.

H/M[8 marks]
Q1. (a) State Zipf's Law and explain its significance for IR. [4] (b) Explain how IDF is derived from Zipf's Law observations. [4]
(a) Zipf: r·f ≈ k (rank × frequency ≈ constant). Consequences for IR: (1) very high-frequency words are stopwords — poor discriminators [1]; (2) many hapax legomena are unhelpful for retrieval [1]; (3) medium-frequency words are most useful for discriminating documents [1]; (4) vocabulary growth is bounded (Heaps) [1]. (b) Zipf shows that terms appearing in many documents have low discriminative value. IDF operationalises this: log(N/D_k) gives high weight to rare terms (small D_k) and low weight to frequent terms (large D_k). When D_k≈N, IDF≈0; when D_k is small, IDF is high [4 marks for correct formula, explanation of N and D_k, worked example].

H/M[12 marks]
Q3. (a) State the BM25 formula and explain the role of each parameter. [6] (b) A document d has length 50 words (avgdl=200). Query term t has f=2 in d, D_k=100, N=10000. Compute the BM25 score for term t alone using k₁=1.2, b=0.75. [4] (c) Explain why BM25 may violate the term-matching guarantee and how BM25+ addresses this. [2]
(a) BM25(d,q) = Σk∈q log((N-D_k+0.5)/(D_k+0.5)) × (k₁+1)×fkd / (k₁×[(1-b)+b×dl/avgdl]+fkd). IDF: log((N-D_k+0.5)/(D_k+0.5)) — probabilistic IDF, penalises common terms [1]. TF component: saturating function of raw tf — prevents term spamming, ceiling = k₁+1 [1]. k₁ (1.2–2): controls saturation speed; higher = slower saturation [1]. b (0.75): length normalisation strength; b=0 = no norm, b=1 = full norm [1]. avgdl: pivot for length normalisation [1]. Sums over query terms [1]. (b) IDF = log((10000-100+0.5)/(100+0.5)) = log(9900.5/100.5) = log(98.51) ≈ 4.590. Norm = (1-0.75)+0.75×(50/200) = 0.25+0.1875 = 0.4375. TF = (2.2×2)/(1.2×0.4375+2) = 4.4/(0.525+2) = 4.4/2.525 ≈ 1.742. Score = 4.590×1.742 = 7.996. [4 marks] (c) For very long documents, the denominator (k₁×norm) becomes very large. A single occurrence may yield near-zero TF contribution — potentially lower than documents not containing the term at all, which violates monotonicity. BM25+ adds δ (typically 1) to the TF component, guaranteeing every matching term contributes at least δ×IDF to the score. [2 marks]
H/M[10 marks]
Q4. Explain BM25F. Why is it better than concatenating all document fields? Describe the pseudo-TF calculation step. Give an example with title and body fields for a web search scenario.
BM25F extends BM25 to structured documents with multiple fields (title, body, URL, anchor text) [1]. Better than concatenation because: (1) fields have different levels of importance — concatenation ignores this [1]; (2) per-field length normalisation is more accurate — a title of 10 words vs a body of 500 words should be normalised separately, not against combined length [1]; (3) field weights w_s directly encode domain knowledge about which fields matter more [1]. Pseudo-TF calculation: for each term k, ˜f_kd = Σs w_s × f_kds / [(1-b_s)+b_s×dl_s/avgdl_s]. Each field's TF is normalised by that field's own average length, then weighted by w_s [2]. Web example: query "climate change". Title field (w=8, b=0.3): f=1 in short 8-word title, avgdl_title=10 → norm=0.3×8/10+0.7=0.94, contribution=8×1/0.94=8.51. Body field (w=1, b=0.75): f=5 in 400-word body, avgdl_body=300 → norm=0.25+0.75×400/300=1.25, contribution=1×5/1.25=4.00. Pseudo-TF = 8.51+4.00=12.51. Then apply saturation: (2.2×12.51)/(1.2+12.51)≈2.0. Multiply by IDF("climate") to get term contribution [4 marks for correct formula, parameter explanation, worked example].
H/M[10 marks]
Q5. (a) Define TF saturation and document length normalisation as used in BM25. Why is each needed? [6] (b) Show the effect of k₁ on TF saturation: for k₁=1.2 and k₁=100, compute the BM25 TF component for f=1, f=5, f=20 (assume norm=1). What does this reveal? [4]
(a) TF saturation: BM25 replaces raw tf with (k₁+1)×tf/(k₁+tf) — a concave function that approaches k₁+1 as tf→∞ [1]. Needed because: raw tf grows without bound, but a document mentioning "neural" 20 times is not 20× more relevant than one mentioning it once; saturation prevents term-spam advantage [2]. Length normalisation: norm = (1-b)+b×dl/avgdl — documents longer than average have their tf penalised [1]. Needed because: longer documents naturally accumulate more term occurrences just due to length — without normalisation, longer documents would be unfairly favoured [2]. (b) k₁=1.2: f=1 → 2.2/2.2=1.0; f=5 → 11/6.2=1.77; f=20 → 44/21.2=2.08. Ceiling = 2.2. k₁=100: f=1 → 101/101=1.0; f=5 → 505/105=4.81; f=20 → 2020/120=16.83. Almost linear (no saturation). Large k₁ behaves like raw TF; small k₁ saturates quickly. k₁=1.2 means even f=5 vs f=20 gives very similar scores, while k₁=100 still heavily rewards higher tf [4 marks].
H/M[8 marks]
Q6. (a) Explain the Robertson-Sparck Jones (RSJ) probabilistic framework for term weighting. Define p and q in this context. [4] (b) What is the probabilistic IDF, and how does it relate to the classic IDF = log(N/D_k)? Under what assumption do they coincide? [4]
(a) RSJ (1976) asks: what weight should term k receive given relevance information? p = P(term k present | document is relevant); q = P(term k present | document is non-relevant). The weight is the log-odds ratio: w_k = log[p_k(1-q_k)/(q_k(1-p_k))]. If p>q, the term is a positive indicator of relevance and gets positive weight [4]. (b) Probabilistic IDF: when no relevance information is available, assume equal prior relevance (all docs equally likely to be relevant), so p_k ≈ D_k/N and q_k ≈ D_k/N. After algebraic simplification with 0.5 smoothing: w_k = log((N-D_k+0.5)/(D_k+0.5)). This coincides approximately with classic IDF = log(N/D_k) when D_k << N (rare terms). The probabilistic version handles edge cases better via 0.5 Laplace smoothing — avoids log(0) when D_k=0 or D_k=N [4].
Flashcards
Zipf's Law
r·f ≈ k: the rank of a word times its frequency is approximately constant. A power law (slope −1 on log-log plot).
IDF
Inverse Document Frequency = log(N/D_k). Measures how rare/specific a term is in the collection. Rare terms get high IDF.
TF-IDF
w = tf × idf. High weight when term appears often in a doc AND is rare in the collection. Foundation of most IR weighting.
Heaps' Law
V = K·n^β (β≈0.5). Vocabulary size grows sub-linearly with corpus size. Lexicon grows as square root of corpus.
Hapax Legomena
Words that appear only once in the corpus. ~50% of vocabulary by Zipf. Poor for retrieval — no statistical signal.
Sublinear TF
(1+log(tf))×idf. Moderates extreme tf values to prevent spam and repeated-term dominance.
Resolving Power
The ability of a term to discriminate relevant from non-relevant documents. Peaks at medium frequency (Luhn 1957).
BM25 k₁
TF saturation parameter (typical: 1.2 or 2.0). k₁→0: binary. k₁→∞: raw TF. k₁+1 is the TF ceiling.
BM25 b
Length normalisation (typical: 0.75). b=0: no length norm. b=1: full length norm. Controls verbosity penalty.
BM25+
BM25 + δ lower bound on TF component. Guarantees documents matching a query term always score higher than those that don't.
BM25F
Field-weighted BM25. Pseudo-TF = Σ w_s × f_kds / norm_s. Per-field length norm before saturation. Used in web search.
Pivoted Norm
Singhal 1996. norm = (1-b)+b·dl/avgdl. Pivot = avgdl. Corrects cosine norm bias against long relevant documents.
RSJ Framework
Robertson & Sparck Jones 1976. Weight = log-odds ratio of term being present in relevant vs non-relevant docs. Basis for probabilistic IDF.
Probabilistic IDF
log((N-D_k+0.5)/(D_k+0.5)). With Laplace smoothing 0.5. Used in BM25. Avoids log(0) and handles edge cases.
TF Saturation
BM25 TF = (k₁+1)·f/(k₁·norm+f). Concave — diminishing returns. A document mentioning term 20× is only slightly more relevant than 10×.
