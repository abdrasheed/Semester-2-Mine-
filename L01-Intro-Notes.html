What is Information Retrieval?

Classic Definitions

Salton (1968):

"Information retrieval is a field concerned with the structure, analysis, organisation, storage, searching and retrieval of information."

Needham (1977):

"The complexity arises from the impossibility of describing the content of a document, or the intent of a request, precisely or unambiguously."

General Definition:

Retrieval of relevant information from data sources which were not originally intended for structured access (e.g., unstructured text, images, video, audio).

🔍 What IR Covers

  • Text documents (most common)
  • Web pages
  • Images and video
  • Audio content
  • Emails, tweets, social media
  • Medical records

🎯 Two Core Goals

  • Effectively: Get the right information to users
  • Efficiently: Get it to users quickly
  • Balance is crucial — often a trade-off
  • IR is the science behind search engines

The IR System — High Level

Data, Information & Knowledge

The Hierarchy

A crucial distinction that motivates why IR is needed:

Data

Raw facts and figures. No context. E.g., the number 37.5 or the string "fever". Databases store data.

Information

Data in context — meaningful data. E.g., "Patient has a fever of 37.5°C". Both databases and IR systems produce information. IR systems also contain information (unlike DBs that contain data).

Knowledge

A basis for making decisions. Going beyond information to actionable understanding. E.g., "A fever of 37.5°C in an adult requires monitoring." This is what a user wants after IR.

Why This Matters for IR

Databases contain data and produce information. IR systems both contain information and provide it. The ultimate goal of IR is to help users obtain knowledge — to satisfy their information need so they can make decisions or take action. This is why ranking and relevance matter so much: retrieving information that doesn't serve the user's knowledge gap is useless.

Historical Timeline of IR

Key Milestones

IR is an old field — much older than Google. Understanding the history explains why current techniques exist.

1940s–1950s: The Origins

Vannevar Bush's memex (1945) — a conceptual machine for storing and cross-referencing documents. Considered the ancestor of hypertext. Mortimer Taube's "uniterm" concept (1951) laid early groundwork for keyword indexing.

1957–1966: Cranfield Experiments

Cyril Cleverdon led the Cranfield experiments at Cranfield Institute of Technology (UK). A landmark: the first systematic, controlled evaluation of IR systems. Used a small test collection of ~1,400 aeronautical engineering abstracts. Key finding: the inverted index is the most effective indexing method. Introduced Precision and Recall as evaluation measures. Also studied stemming effectiveness for the first time.

1960s–1970s: Theoretical Foundations

Gerard Salton developed the Vector Space Model (SMART system, Cornell, 1960s) — representing documents and queries as vectors in term space. Karen Sparck Jones introduced IDF (Inverse Document Frequency) weighting (1972), showing rare terms are more informative. Robertson & Jones formulated the Probability Ranking Principle (1977).

1978: Swets ROC Analysis

Swets (1963/1969) applied ROC curve analysis to IR, providing a theoretical framework for comparing retrieval systems. His work established that a system ranking documents by estimated probability of relevance is optimal — foundational to the PRP.

1992–present: TREC

The Text REtrieval Conference (TREC), run by NIST (USA), began in 1992. Scaled up Cranfield's approach to millions of documents. Standardised test collections, relevance assessments by human judges, and metrics (MAP, NDCG). Enabled reproducible comparison of systems worldwide. TREC tracks have covered Web IR, genomics, legal, medical, and more.

1994–1998: Web IR & PageRank

World Wide Web created a new IR challenge at web scale. AltaVista, Lycos, Excite — early web search engines. Larry Page & Sergey Brin published PageRank (1998): using hyperlink graph to estimate page authority. Google launched — today one of the largest companies in the world, built entirely on IR.

2013–present: Neural IR

Word2Vec (2013), BERT (2018), and transformer-based models revolutionised IR. Dense retrieval, cross-encoders, and neural ranking models. GPT-based RAG systems (2020s) create a new paradigm where IR feeds LLM generation.

Cranfield by the Numbers

  • ~1,400 aeronautical engineering abstracts
  • ~225 queries with relevance judgements
  • Conducted in two phases: Cranfield I (1957) and Cranfield II (1963–66)
  • Cranfield II tested 33 different index languages (stemming variants, thesaurus, etc.)
  • Key finding: simple keyword indexing with stemming outperforms complex thesauri
  • First use of Precision and Recall as IR evaluation metrics

IR vs. Database Retrieval

🗄️ Database / SQL

  • Structured query language (SQL)
  • Well-defined fields/attributes
  • Exact match semantics
  • Deterministic — always a "right" answer
  • E.g. SELECT * WHERE account = 123

🔍 Information Retrieval

  • Unstructured natural language queries
  • Free-form documents
  • Approximate / ranked results
  • Heuristic — no single "right" answer
  • E.g. "find documents about hippos in the zoo"

⚠️ Key Insight: IR is Heuristic

Most algorithms in Computer Science have a definitive "right" answer. IR is fundamentally different — we use heuristics because we cannot know with certainty which documents are relevant to a given user's need. Consider: sorting integers has one answer; finding "relevant" documents does not.

Information Needs & Query Types

What is an Information Need?

An information need is the underlying cause of the query submitted to a search engine. It can be vague, ambiguous, or hard to express. The same query string may represent very different information needs by different users.

🧭 Navigational

Reach a specific website or URL already in mind. E.g.: "University of Glasgow homepage"

📖 Informational

Acquire general information about a topic. E.g.: "How do vaccines work?"

🛒 Transactional

Reach a site for further interaction (shopping, download). E.g.: "buy iPhone 16"

🔍 Ad Hoc Search

Find documents "about topic X". The classic IR task — new queries on a static collection.

🎯 Known-Item Search

Find one specific document. E.g. "University of Glasgow home page". Measured with MRR.

Example: The Same Query, Different Needs

Consider the query ash:

  • A geologist may want: ash cloud from a volcano
  • A Game of Thrones fan may want: the character Ash
  • A music fan may want: the band Ash

Over 16% of web queries are ambiguous!

The Retrieval Process

Three-Part Retrieval Pipeline

IR System Capabilities

Given few query words, an IR system must:

  • Infer what the user wants (handle vague queries)
  • Fetch relevant documents as fast as possible
  • Present results in a way users understand
  • Support query reformulation and feedback

Relevance, Effectiveness & Efficiency

📌 Relevance

A document is topically relevant if it and the query are about the same topic. User relevance is broader (novelty, freshness, authority, etc.)

📊 Effectiveness

The quality of the retrieved set — do results largely contain relevant documents? Measured by Precision, Recall, NDCG, MAP.

⚡ Efficiency

Return results as fast as possible. Role of indexing, caching, compression. Even a 0.2s delay can hurt user satisfaction.

What Makes a Document Relevant?

Content signals:

  • Contains the query terms
  • Contains ALL query terms
  • Query terms appear often (high TF)
  • Query terms appear in a short document
  • Query terms appear close together
  • Contains synonyms of query terms

Authority/other signals:

  • Document is authoritative (many links)
  • Not too many ads/spam
  • Clicked by many users for this query
  • Fresh / recent content
  • Not too many unrelated words

🔑 All of these are heuristics!

None are guaranteed to correctly identify relevant documents for all users and all queries.

The Probability Ranking Principle (PRP)

Robertson (1977) — The Theoretical Foundation of Ranked Retrieval

The Probability Ranking Principle (Robertson, 1977):

"If a reference retrieval system's response to each request is a ranking of the documents in the collection in order of decreasing probability of relevance to the request, where the probabilities are estimated as accurately as possible on the basis of whatever data have been made available to the system for this purpose, the overall effectiveness of the system to its users will be the best that is obtainable on the basis of those data."

In plain English: rank documents by their probability of being relevant, highest first. This is the theoretical justification for ranked retrieval and forms the basis of probabilistic IR models (e.g., BM25, language models).

Why PRP is Important

  • Provides a theoretical optimality argument for ranking
  • Justifies why ranked retrieval is better than Boolean retrieval
  • Bridges IR and probability theory
  • Basis for BM25 (Robertson & Walker, 1994)
  • Still the gold standard principle for ranking today

PRP Assumptions

  • Documents are assessed independently of each other
  • Probabilities are estimated from available data
  • User examines documents in ranked order (top-down)
  • Independence assumption is unrealistic but practical
  • Violated by: duplicate documents, complementary documents

Formal Statement

Given a query q and a document d, the PRP says rank documents by:

\[ P(R=1 \mid d, q) \]

where \(R=1\) means the document is relevant. Any ranking score that is a monotonically increasing function of this probability achieves the same optimal ranking. The Odds Ratio form is often used:

\[ \text{Odds}(R=1 \mid d,q) = \frac{P(R=1 \mid d,q)}{P(R=0 \mid d,q)} \]

Ranking by odds gives the same ordering as ranking by probability — and is often mathematically more convenient (leads to log-odds, i.e., BM25-style scoring).

PRP vs. Heuristic Models

Not all IR models explicitly estimate \(P(R=1 \mid d,q)\). TF-IDF and cosine similarity are heuristic scores that correlate with relevance but lack a formal probabilistic derivation. BM25 is derived more rigorously from the Robertson–Spark Jones probability model, making it more theoretically grounded.

The Cranfield Evaluation Paradigm

How IR Systems are Evaluated

The Cranfield paradigm (derived from the Cranfield experiments) defines how we measure IR system effectiveness. It is the standard methodology used by TREC and all modern IR evaluation.

1. Test Collection

A static set of documents. E.g., Cranfield: 1,400 abstracts; TREC: millions of news/web documents.

2. Topic Set

A set of queries (topics) with formal descriptions of the user's information need. TREC topics include Title, Description, and Narrative fields.

3. Relevance Judgements (Qrels)

Human assessors judge each (query, document) pair as relevant or not. This "ground truth" is used to compute metrics. Often binary (relevant/not) or graded (0/1/2/3).

The Evaluation Loop

  1. System retrieves a ranked list of documents for each query
  2. Compare ranked list against relevance judgements (qrels)
  3. Compute metrics: Precision, Recall, MAP, NDCG, etc.
  4. Average metrics across all queries → overall system score
  5. Compare systems — highest score wins

Key Metrics (Preview — details in L05)

  • Precision@k: fraction of top-k retrieved docs that are relevant
  • Recall: fraction of all relevant docs that were retrieved
  • MAP: Mean Average Precision — average precision at each relevant doc position, averaged over queries
  • NDCG: Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain — accounts for graded relevance and rank position

Pooling (TREC Method)

Since judging every document is impossible for large collections, TREC uses pooling: take the top-K results from each participating system, pool them together, and only judge documents in the pool. Unjudged documents are assumed non-relevant. This makes evaluation feasible at scale.

Critique of Cranfield

  • Relevance is treated as binary — oversimplified
  • Judgements by one assessor — inter-annotator agreement ~60-75%
  • Static collections don't reflect web dynamics
  • Does not capture user satisfaction directly
  • Despite limitations, still the gold standard methodology

Worked Example: Precision and Recall

Collection of 10 documents. Query has 4 truly relevant documents (R = {d1, d3, d5, d8}). System returns ranked list: [d1, d2, d3, d6, d5].

  • Precision@5 = (relevant in top 5) / 5 = 3/5 = 0.6
  • Recall@5 = (relevant in top 5) / (total relevant) = 3/4 = 0.75
  • Average Precision = (P@1 + P@3 + P@5) / 4 = (1 + 2/3 + 3/5) / 4 = (1 + 0.667 + 0.6) / 4 = 0.567

Imprecision & Heuristics in IR

The "Right Answer" Problem

Algorithms WITH a right answer:

  • Sort these 10 integers → one correct order
  • Find the maximum value → one answer
  • SELECT name FROM users WHERE id=5 → exact result

IR — NO single right answer:

  • "Find documents most relevant to hippos in the zoo"
  • Different users have different needs
  • Relevance is subjective
  • We use heuristics that approximate the right answer

Example: Imprecision in Practice

A query for "bank" could mean a riverbank, a financial institution, or a data bank. The IR system must guess which meaning the user intends — often using context clues, click data, location, and prior queries.

RAG = LLM + IR system

Summary

IR Definition

IR is the science of finding relevant information in unstructured data sources. It powers search engines, AI assistants, and enterprise search tools.

Heuristic Nature

IR techniques are heuristics — there is no single "correct" answer. Relevance is subjective and context-dependent.

Three Core Concerns

Relevance (what is relevant?), Effectiveness (quality of results), Efficiency (speed of retrieval). All three interact and trade off.

The Pipeline

IR systems have three main parts: (1) Text Processing, (2) Indexing, (3) Retrieval. Each has distinct algorithms and data structures.

Short Answer Questions
Q1. What is the difference between "Information" and "Data"?
Information is data in context. Databases contain data and produce information. IR systems both contain and provide information. Knowledge goes further — it is a basis for making decisions.
Q2. Give three examples of signals that might indicate a document is relevant to a query.
Any three from: (1) contains all query terms, (2) query terms appear frequently (high TF), (3) query terms appear close together, (4) document is authoritative (many inlinks), (5) document has been clicked by many users for that query, (6) contains terms similar to query terms, (7) document is fresh/recent.
Q3. What are the three types of queries according to Broder's taxonomy?
(1) Navigational — to reach a specific website already in mind; (2) Informational — to acquire information on a topic; (3) Transactional — to reach a site for further interaction such as shopping or downloading.
Q4. Why is the "bag of words" model useful despite ignoring word order?
Despite ignoring word order, bag-of-words is simple to compute, works well in practice for topical retrieval, and allows efficient indexing. Statistical properties of term frequencies provide good approximations of document content. The model is foundational to TF-IDF and BM25, which remain highly effective.
Q5. What is Belkin's ASK hypothesis and why is it significant for IR?
The Anomalous State of Knowledge (ASK) hypothesis (Belkin et al., 1982) states that information needs are difficult to define because they represent a gap in knowledge. Since users don't yet know what they're looking for, they can't specify it precisely. This motivates interactive IR, query expansion, and relevance feedback as ways to iteratively refine what the user needs.
Q6. Distinguish between "effectiveness" and "efficiency" in an IR system.
Effectiveness concerns the quality of retrieved results — how many relevant documents are found and how highly they rank (measured by Precision, Recall, MAP, NDCG). Efficiency concerns the speed of retrieval — how quickly results are returned (measured by query latency, throughput). They often trade off: more complex models improve effectiveness but require more computation.
Q7. What is an inverted index and why is it needed?
An inverted index maps terms to the list of documents that contain them (a term → document mapping). It is needed because without it, a system would need to scan all documents for every query — infeasible for billions of documents. With an inverted index, retrieval only processes the posting lists of query terms, giving sub-second response times.
Q8. Name four application domains of information retrieval beyond web search.
Any four from: (1) Email search, (2) Medical record retrieval, (3) Enterprise/intranet search, (4) Social media search (Twitter, LinkedIn), (5) Digital library search (ACM, PubMed), (6) News search, (7) Legal document retrieval, (8) Patent search, (9) Image/video retrieval, (10) AI assistants using RAG.
Long Answer / Essay Questions
Q1. Compare and contrast Information Retrieval with traditional database retrieval. In your answer, discuss the nature of queries, data representations, matching mechanisms, and the concept of relevance. [10 marks]
Database Retrieval: Uses structured query languages (SQL). Data is organised into tables with well-defined fields (attributes). Queries use exact match semantics (WHERE clause). The system always returns a deterministic, correct answer. Relevance is binary and objective — either the record matches the criteria or not. Example: SELECT * FROM accounts WHERE id=12345.

Information Retrieval: Queries are natural language (or keyword). Data is unstructured (free-form text, images, etc.). Matching is approximate — the system ranks documents by estimated relevance. There is no single "correct" answer; relevance is subjective and depends on context, user, and task. Techniques are heuristic.

Key differences: (1) Data type: structured vs. unstructured; (2) Query type: formal vs. natural language; (3) Matching: exact vs. approximate; (4) Output: exact result set vs. ranked list; (5) Correctness: deterministic vs. probabilistic/heuristic. The imprecision in IR arises from the difficulty of describing document content and query intent unambiguously (Needham, 1977).
Q2. Explain the three-part pipeline of a typical IR system (text processing, indexing, retrieval) and describe the purpose of each stage. [8 marks]
Part 1 — Text Processing: Input documents are processed to create concise representations. Steps include: Tokenisation (splitting text into words), Stopword Removal (removing high-frequency, low-discriminative words like "the", "and"), and Stemming (reducing words to their root forms, e.g., "connecting" → "connect"). Output: {docID, term, frequency} triplets.

Part 2 — Indexing: The processed representations are stored in a fast-lookup data structure — the inverted index. This is a term → documents mapping. The inverted index enables fast retrieval by only processing relevant posting lists during query time, rather than scanning all documents.

Part 3 — Retrieval: Given a user query (also processed through tokenisation/stemming), the system searches the inverted index, retrieves posting lists for query terms, computes a relevance score for each document using a retrieval model (e.g., TF-IDF, BM25), and returns a ranked list of documents to the user. Users may provide feedback to refine results.
Q3. Discuss the role of Information Retrieval in modern AI systems, with particular reference to Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). [6 marks]
Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT and Claude can generate fluent text but are prone to "hallucinations" — generating plausible but incorrect information. RAG addresses this by integrating IR with LLMs: when a user poses a question, an IR system first retrieves relevant documents from a corpus; these documents are then provided as context to the LLM, which generates an answer grounded in the retrieved evidence. Benefits: (1) reduces hallucinations by grounding generation in retrieved facts; (2) allows attribution of answers to source documents; (3) enables LLMs to access up-to-date information without retraining. This has sparked a renaissance in IR research, as the quality of retrieval directly impacts LLM output quality. Enterprise IR, scientific literature search, and legal document retrieval are key RAG application areas.

Q4. Describe the three-part pipeline of an IR system. For each part, explain its purpose and list its main operations. Give an example of how the query "running shoes" would be processed at each stage.
Part 1 — Text Processing [3 marks]: Purpose: create concise, normalized term representations. Operations: (a) Tokenisation: "running shoes" → [running, shoes]; (b) Stopword removal: no change here; (c) Stemming: [run, shoe]. Applied to both documents and queries.

Documents containing both stemmed terms in appropriate frequencies rank highest.
M[6 marks]
Q5. Define and distinguish between "relevance", "effectiveness", and "efficiency" in the context of Information Retrieval systems.
Relevance [2 marks]: A document is relevant if it satisfies the user's information need. Topical relevance means query and document are on the same topic; user relevance additionally considers novelty, freshness, authority. Relevance is subjective and context-dependent. Effectiveness [2 marks]: The quality of the retrieved result set — how many relevant documents are retrieved and how highly they rank. Measured by Precision, Recall, MAP, NDCG. A highly effective system finds and ranks relevant documents accurately. Efficiency [2 marks]: The speed and resource usage of retrieval. Measured by query latency and throughput. Critical for user satisfaction — delays over 0.2s reduce perceived quality. The three are related: improving effectiveness (e.g., with re-ranking) often reduces efficiency, requiring infrastructure trade-offs.
H[8 marks]
Q6. State the Probability Ranking Principle (Robertson, 1977). Explain what it claims, what assumptions it makes, and why it is important for the theory of ranked retrieval. Discuss one limitation of the PRP in practice.
Statement [2 marks]: The PRP states that ranking documents in decreasing order of their estimated probability of relevance \(P(R=1|d,q)\) gives the optimal retrieval effectiveness achievable from the available data.

What it claims [1 mark]: That ranked retrieval (highest probability first) is theoretically optimal — no other ordering can do better in expectation.

Assumptions [2 marks]: (1) Documents are assessed independently of each other — the relevance of one document does not depend on which other documents are retrieved. (2) Users examine results in ranked order from top to bottom. (3) Probabilities are estimated from available data (queries, document features).

Importance [2 marks]: Provides the theoretical justification for all probabilistic IR models, including BM25. Bridges probability theory and IR practice. Explains why ranking is better than Boolean retrieval.

Limitation [1 mark]: The independence assumption is violated in practice — if document A and document B are near-duplicates, retrieving both wastes results. Similarly, complementary documents (one gives half the answer) would be better retrieved together, not independently ranked.
H/M[10 marks]
Q7. Describe the Cranfield evaluation paradigm. What are its three components? Explain how TREC extended this paradigm to web-scale collections. Discuss two limitations of the Cranfield approach and how they affect the validity of IR evaluation results.
Three Components [3 marks]: (1) Test Collection: a static, fixed set of documents (Cranfield: ~1,400 abstracts; TREC: millions of documents). (2) Topic Set: a set of queries with formal information need descriptions (TREC topics have Title, Description, Narrative). (3) Relevance Judgements (Qrels): human assessors judge each (query, document) pair as relevant or not — this provides the ground truth for computing metrics.

TREC Extension [3 marks]: TREC (from 1992, NIST) scaled Cranfield to millions of documents using pooling — top-K results from all participating systems are pooled, and only pooled documents are judged. This makes large-scale evaluation feasible. TREC standardises topics and metrics (MAP, NDCG), enabling reproducible cross-system comparison. Hundreds of groups worldwide submit runs annually.

Limitation 1 [2 marks]: Binary relevance — treating documents as simply relevant or not is an oversimplification. In reality, relevance is graded (highly relevant, partially relevant, not relevant). Graded metrics like NDCG partially address this, but binary qrels still dominate. This can mean two systems performing identically on binary metrics but differently for user satisfaction.

Limitation 2 [2 marks]: Inter-assessor disagreement — different humans judge the same document differently. Studies show assessors agree only ~60-75% of the time. This means the "ground truth" qrels are themselves noisy, making evaluation results unreliable at the margins. Additionally, unjudged documents are assumed non-relevant (pooling assumption), which may unfairly penalise systems that retrieve many relevant unjudged documents.
H/M[6 marks]
Q8. Distinguish between "data", "information", and "knowledge" in the context of IR. Why is this distinction important for understanding the goals of an IR system?
Data [1 mark]: Raw facts without context. E.g., the number 37.5 or the string "fever". Databases store data. On its own, data is not useful to a user without interpretation.

Information [2 marks]: Data in context — data that has been given meaning. E.g., "Patient temperature is 37.5°C — a fever." Databases produce information from data. IR systems both contain information (their documents are meaningful) and provide information to users. This is a key difference from databases.

Knowledge [1 mark]: A basis for making decisions. Knowing that a 37.5°C fever in an adult needs monitoring. Knowledge requires interpretation and context beyond raw information.

Importance for IR [2 marks]: The ultimate goal of IR is to help users obtain knowledge to satisfy their information need. Simply returning a document (data) is insufficient — the system must return the right document (information) that enables the user to gain knowledge. This is why relevance matters — a retrieved document that doesn't address the user's knowledge gap fails its purpose, even if it technically "matches" the query. This distinction also explains why relevance is not binary but context-dependent.
H[8 marks]
Q9. Describe the history of Information Retrieval from the Cranfield experiments through to the modern neural IR era. Identify at least four key milestones and explain the significance of each.
Any four milestones well described, 2 marks each (up to 8):

Cranfield Experiments (1957–1966) [2 marks]: First systematic, controlled evaluation of IR systems. Introduced Precision and Recall. Tested 33 indexing strategies. Key finding: simple keyword indexing with stemming outperforms complex thesauri. Established the test-collection-based evaluation paradigm.

Vector Space Model / IDF (1960s–1972) [2 marks]: Salton's SMART system introduced the Vector Space Model — representing documents and queries as vectors. Sparck Jones (1972) introduced Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) — rare terms are more informative. This gave us TF-IDF, still widely used.

Probability Ranking Principle (Robertson, 1977) [2 marks]: Theoretical foundation for ranked retrieval. Ranking by estimated probability of relevance is optimal. Led to BM25 (1994), the most widely used ranking function.

TREC (1992–present) [2 marks]: Scaled Cranfield evaluation to millions of documents. Standardised test collections, metrics, and evaluation methodology. Enabled reproducible worldwide comparison of IR systems.

PageRank / Google (1998) [2 marks]: Used hyperlink graph to estimate page authority. Combined with TF-IDF-style content matching to create web-scale IR. Commercialised IR and enabled the modern web economy.

Neural IR / BERT / RAG (2013–present) [2 marks]: Dense neural representations replaced sparse bag-of-words. Transformer models (BERT, GPT) enable semantic matching. RAG combines IR with LLM generation for grounded AI answers.

Critical Thinking Questions
Q1. Should a search engine prioritise a user's immediate stated query or try to infer their deeper information need? What are the risks of each approach?
Immediate query: safer, more transparent, but may miss what user really needs (Belkin's ASK). Inferred need: more helpful if correct, but risky — may be wrong or feel intrusive/paternalistic. Risk of filter bubble. Balance: serve the stated query well, offer query suggestions to help users refine their need. Modern engines use click data and context to infer intent without explicitly overriding the query.
Q2. Is it possible to build a completely objective IR system? Why or why not?
No — relevance judgments by humans are subjective and context-dependent (even trained TREC assessors disagree ~25% of the time). The weighting and ranking functions embed design choices (e.g., how much to weight title vs body). Training data reflects historical user behaviour which has biases. Objectivity is an ideal rather than an achievable state in IR.
Q3. How might IR systems need to evolve as AI assistants become the primary way users access information?
IR must support: (1) multi-hop reasoning (AI asks multiple sub-queries); (2) returning evidence chunks rather than full documents; (3) grading quality/trustworthiness of sources; (4) real-time indexing for currency; (5) multi-modal retrieval (images, audio for AI). The "user" may be an LLM, not a human, changing what "relevant" and "useful" mean.

Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and reveal the definition.

Information Retrieval
Field concerned with structure, analysis, organisation, storage, searching, and retrieval of information from unstructured sources.
Heuristic
A technique that tries to approximate the right answer rather than compute it exactly. All IR ranking functions are heuristics because relevance is subjective.
Topical Relevance
A document is topically relevant to a query if both concern the same topic.
Inverted Index
A data structure mapping each term to a list of documents containing that term. Enables fast query processing.
ASK Hypothesis
Anomalous State of Knowledge (Belkin 1982): users find it hard to articulate their information need because it represents a gap in their knowledge.
RAG
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: combining LLMs with IR to ground generated text in retrieved documents, reducing hallucinations.
Navigational Query
A query issued to reach a specific website or URL that the user already has in mind. E.g., "University of Glasgow".
Informational Query
A query issued to acquire information on a topic. Reading/bookmarking the result is the primary action.
Transactional Query
A query where the user intends to interact further with the found website (shopping, downloading, gaming).
Effectiveness
Quality of retrieval results — how many relevant documents are found and how highly ranked they are. Measured by P, R, MAP, NDCG.
Efficiency
Speed of retrieval — how quickly results are returned. Even 0.2s delays affect user satisfaction. Role of inverted index, caching, compression.
Bag of Words
Document representation that counts term occurrences, ignoring word order. Basis for TF-IDF and BM25. Simple yet effective.
Probability Ranking Principle
Robertson (1977): rank documents by P(R=1|d,q) — probability of relevance — highest first. Theoretically optimal retrieval strategy. Basis of BM25.
Cranfield Experiments
First systematic IR evaluation (Cleverdon, 1957–1966). ~1,400 aeronautical abstracts. Introduced Precision, Recall, and the test-collection evaluation paradigm.
TREC
Text REtrieval Conference (NIST, since 1992). Scales Cranfield to millions of docs. Uses pooling for relevance judgements. Standardises MAP, NDCG metrics.
IDF (Sparck Jones, 1972)
Inverse Document Frequency: rarer terms are more informative. IDF = log(N/df). Basis of TF-IDF weighting — rare query terms should count more.
Pooling (TREC)
To make large-scale evaluation feasible: take top-K results from each participating system, pool them, and only judge pooled documents. Unjudged docs assumed non-relevant.
Data vs Information
Data: raw facts (e.g., 37.5). Information: data in context (e.g., "patient has 37.5°C fever"). IR systems both contain and provide information; DBs contain data and produce information.
Ad Hoc Search
Classic IR task: find documents about topic X from a static collection. New queries arrive over time. Evaluated by TREC. Different from known-item search.
Relevance Judgements (Qrels)
Human assessors judge (query, document) pairs as relevant or not. Used as ground truth in Cranfield-style evaluation. Often binary, sometimes graded (0–3).
