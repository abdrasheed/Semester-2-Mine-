Hidden Markov Models (HMM)

HMMs extend Naive Bayes to sequences, modelling how tags depend on each other.

Core Components

Observed nodes xi

The words we can see.

Hidden nodes yi

The POS tags we want to predict.

Transition probabilities

\( P(y_i \mid y_{i-1}) \)

Probability of tag given the previous tag (horizontal arrows).

Emission probabilities

\( P(x_i \mid y_i) \)

Probability of a word given its tag (vertical arrows).

The Markov Assumption

Key Assumption: The current state depends only on the previous state.
\( P(y_i \mid y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_{i-1}) = P(y_i \mid y_{i-1}) \)

A 2nd-order HMM would condition on the previous 2 states.

HMM Architecture Diagram

Purple arrows = Transitions  |  Gold dashed = Emissions

Training: Count and Normalise

From manually labelled (treebank) data, we estimate:

\[ P(y_i \mid y_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(y_{i-1}, y_i)}{\text{count}(y_{i-1})} \]
\[ P(x_i \mid y_i) = \frac{\text{count}(y_i, x_i)}{\text{count}(y_i)} \]

Inference: Sequence Score

The probability of a complete tag sequence and word sequence:

\[ P(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(y_i \mid y_{i-1}) \cdot P(x_i \mid y_i) \]

Greedy Approach (Suboptimal)

Greedy: At each step, pick the most likely tag given the previous decision.
Problem: a locally good choice may lead to a globally bad sequence.

Greedy Step Formula

\[ y_i^* = \arg\max_{y_i} P(y_i \mid y_{i-1}^*) \cdot P(x_i \mid y_i) \]

Exercise: HMM POS Inference on "Sue drew Mark"

Given:

Transitions

  • P(VERB|<START>) = 1/4
  • P(PROPN|<START>) = 3/4
  • P(VERB|VERB) = 0
  • P(PROPN|VERB) = 1
  • P(VERB|PROPN) = 2/4
  • P(PROPN|PROPN) = 2/4

Emissions

  • P(sue|PROPN)=1/6, P(sue|VERB)=2/6
  • P(drew|PROPN)=2/6, P(drew|VERB)=3/6
  • P(mark|PROPN)=3/6, P(mark|VERB)=2/6

Step 1 — "sue":

P(PROPN|<S>)×P(sue|PROPN) = 3/4 × 1/6 = 3/24

P(VERB|<S>)×P(sue|VERB) = 1/4 × 2/6 = 2/24 → Choose PROPN

Step 2 — "drew":

P(VERB|PROPN)×P(drew|VERB) = 2/4 × 3/6 = 6/24

P(PROPN|PROPN)×P(drew|PROPN) = 2/4 × 2/6 = 4/24 → Choose VERB

Step 3 — "mark":

P(PROPN|VERB)×P(mark|PROPN) = 1 × 3/6 = 3/6

P(VERB|VERB)×P(mark|VERB) = 0 → Choose PROPN

Result: PROPN VERB PROPN

HMM-P3 — Domain Mismatch Exam Style — 2022 Q3(c)

An HMM POS tagger trained on news data performs poorly on patent text. (a) Explain why. (b) Propose two fixes.

(a) Why it fails: Domain mismatch — the tagger was trained on general news text which has different vocabulary, sentence structure, and POS distributions from patent text. Specialised patent vocabulary will be unknown to the tagger.

(b) Two fixes:

  1. Manually annotate a sample of patent documents and fine-tune the tagger on this domain-specific training data.
  2. Use a domain-specific dictionary to assign default POS tags to specialised patent terminology before running the tagger.

The Viterbi Algorithm

Key Advantage over Greedy: Viterbi considers the entire sequence simultaneously using dynamic programming. It finds the globally optimal tag sequence — not just locally optimal choices.

Dynamic Programming Intuition

Just like computing Fibonacci numbers efficiently by storing results:

\[ \text{fib}(n) = \text{fib}(n-1) + \text{fib}(n-2) \]

We build up probabilities from left to right, never recomputing the same sub-problem.

Algorithm Steps

  1. Initialise: score each tag for the first word
  2. For each subsequent word: compute max probability of reaching each tag
  3. Track back-pointers (which previous state led to max)
  4. Backtrack from end to recover optimal sequence

Viterbi Recurrence

\[ \delta_i(y) = \max_{y'} \Big[ \delta_{i-1}(y') \cdot P(y \mid y') \Big] \cdot P(x_i \mid y) \]
\[ \psi_i(y) = \arg\max_{y'} \Big[ \delta_{i-1}(y') \cdot P(y \mid y') \Big] \]

Where \(\delta_i(y)\) = max probability of any path ending in state \(y\) at position \(i\), and \(\psi_i(y)\) = back-pointer.

Quick Practice — Viterbi

VIT-P1 — Identify the Recurrence Exam Style

Write the Viterbi recurrence relation for \(\delta_i(y)\) and explain what each term represents.

\[ \delta_i(y) = \max_{y'} \Big[ \delta_{i-1}(y') \cdot P(y \mid y') \Big] \cdot P(x_i \mid y) \]
  • \(\delta_{i-1}(y')\) — the best probability of any path ending in tag \(y'\) at the previous position
  • \(P(y \mid y')\) — transition probability from previous tag \(y'\) to current tag \(y\)
  • \(P(x_i \mid y)\) — emission probability of seeing current word \(x_i\) given tag \(y\)
  • The \(\max\) picks the best predecessor, stored in back-pointer \(\psi_i(y)\)

VIT-P2 — Viterbi Step Calculation Numerical

Given δ₁(NOUN)=0.4, δ₁(VERB)=0.1. Transitions: P(VERB|NOUN)=0.6, P(VERB|VERB)=0.2. Emission: P("runs"|VERB)=0.5. Compute δ₂(VERB).

δ₂(VERB) = max[ δ₁(NOUN) × P(VERB|NOUN), δ₁(VERB) × P(VERB|VERB) ] × P("runs"|VERB)

= max[ 0.4 × 0.6, 0.1 × 0.2 ] × 0.5

= max[ 0.24, 0.02 ] × 0.5

= 0.24 × 0.5 = 0.12

Back-pointer ψ₂(VERB) = NOUN (since 0.24 > 0.02)

VIT-P3 — Why Viterbi is Optimal Conceptual

Explain why the Viterbi algorithm is guaranteed to find the globally optimal POS tag sequence while greedy is not.

Greedy makes locally optimal decisions one word at a time. A locally good choice at position i may prevent an even better global choice — for example, tagging "bank" as NOUN early on may block a better overall sequence where "bank" is VERB.

Viterbi uses the principle of optimality (Bellman): the optimal path to state y at position i must pass through the optimal sub-path ending at position i−1. By storing the best sub-path probability at every (position, tag) pair and propagating forward, it guarantees no better path exists at each step — by induction, the final backtracked sequence is globally optimal.

Transition-Based Parsing

Build a parse tree through a sequence of actions on a stack and buffer.

State Representation

Stack

Words being considered for dependency arcs. Starts with ROOT.

Buffer

Remaining input words. Words shift from buffer onto stack.

Arc-Standard Actions

SHIFT

Pop word from buffer, push to stack.

LEFT-ARC

Add edge: stack top is dependent of buffer front. Remove child from stack.

RIGHT-ARC

Add edge: buffer front is dependent of stack top. Remove child from stack.

Interactive Demo: "Sally likes movies"

Step 1 / 7

Complexity Analysis

For N tokens in a sentence:

  • O(N) SHIFT operations — one per token
  • O(N) REDUCE operations — one per dependency edge
\[ \text{Total: } O(N) \text{ — linear time!} \]
Key Limitation: Actions are irreversible. You cannot undo a wrong arc. So the classifier must choose correctly every time.

Choosing Transitions: ML Classifier

Input: Current state = {stack features, buffer features}

Output: Action scores = {SHIFT, LEFT-ARC, RIGHT-ARC}

\[ \text{action}^* = \arg\max_{a \in \{\text{Shift, L-Arc, R-Arc}\}} \text{score}(a \mid \text{state}) \]

Classifiers used: Rule-based, SVM, Neural Networks

Training signal: Gold (ground truth) actions from annotated treebanks

1. Syntax: How words combine grammatically. E.g., "bank" is the direct object of "ran".

2. Semantics: What words mean. E.g., "bank" = financial institution vs. riverbank.

SA2 — Naive Bayes for POS

Why is Naive Bayes insufficient for POS tagging? Give an example illustrating the problem.

Naive Bayes ignores sequential context — it assigns the same tag to a word regardless of its position or surrounding words.

Example: The word "call" would always be tagged as NOUN (most frequent), but in "Please call your mum" it should be VERB. Naive Bayes cannot distinguish these uses.

SA3 — Dependency Tree Properties

List three formal properties that a valid dependency tree must satisfy.

  1. Every word has exactly one incoming edge (except ROOT which has none).
  2. Weakly connected: replacing all directed edges with undirected edges yields a connected graph.
  3. Acyclic: no cycles exist in the directed graph.

SA4 — HMM Training

Describe how emission probabilities are estimated from a labelled corpus in HMM training. Write the formula.

Count how many times each word appears with each POS tag, then normalise by the total count of that tag:

\[ P(x_i \mid y_i) = \frac{\text{count}(y_i, x_i)}{\text{count}(y_i)} \]

Example: If "ate" appears 50 times total and 50 of those are tagged VERB: P("ate" | VERB) = 50/50 = 1.0

SA5 — Greedy vs Viterbi

Explain intuitively why the greedy approach may fail where Viterbi succeeds. No formulas needed.

The greedy approach picks the locally best tag at each step without considering future words. This can lead to an early commitment that becomes wrong once we see later words.

Analogy: Like choosing the shortest road at each junction in a road trip — you might end up on a fast road that leads to a dead end, while a slightly slower road at that junction led to a faster overall route.

Viterbi keeps track of all possible paths and finds the one with the maximum total probability across the entire sequence.

LA2 — Viterbi Forward Pass

Explain the Viterbi recurrence relation in detail. Describe both the forward pass (computing δ) and the backtracking step. Why is it guaranteed to find the globally optimal sequence?

Forward Pass:

Define \(\delta_i(y)\) as the maximum probability of any tag sequence ending in state \(y\) at position \(i\):

\[ \delta_i(y) = \max_{y'} \Big[ \delta_{i-1}(y') \cdot P(y \mid y') \Big] \cdot P(x_i \mid y) \]

Simultaneously record back-pointers:

\[ \psi_i(y) = \arg\max_{y'} \Big[ \delta_{i-1}(y') \cdot P(y \mid y') \Big] \]

Backtracking: Start from the end state with maximum \(\delta_n\), then follow back-pointers \(\psi\) from right to left to recover the optimal sequence.

Why globally optimal? The DP principle: the optimal path to state \(y\) at position \(i\) must pass through the optimal sub-path ending at the best predecessor. By induction, if we store the best path to each state at each step, we guarantee the globally best path.

LA4 — System Design

You are building a system to extract company-risk relations from news text (e.g., "Apple is facing a fine"). Explain how POS tagging and dependency parsing would help you avoid false positives like "I feel fine, said Tim Cook of Apple."

A simple bag-of-words classifier would see the same tokens ("Apple", "fine", "Tim Cook") in both sentences and incorrectly classify both as expressing company risk.

Using POS tagging: In "Apple is facing a fine", "fine" is tagged NOUN (the risk). In "I feel fine", "fine" is tagged ADJ (a feeling). This alone can disambiguate many cases.

Using dependency parsing: We can check the syntactic role of each word:

  • In the risk sentence: "fine" has the dependency relation dobj with "facing", and "Apple" is the nsubj.
  • In the safe sentence: "fine" is a predicative adjective (acomp) of "feel", with no connection to "Apple" via the risk relation.

By conditioning our relation extractor on these structural features rather than just word presence, we can correctly identify which sentence expresses a company-risk relation.

Concept / Analysis

C1 — Garden Path Sentences

Explain why the sentence "The horse raced past the barn fell" is syntactically valid. What does this reveal about the difficulty of parsing?

The sentence is a reduced relative clause: "The horse [that was] raced past the barn fell." Initially, readers parse "raced" as the main verb, then encounter "fell" and must re-analyse.

Implication for parsing: The parser must consider multiple interpretations simultaneously. This is why chart-based methods (which maintain multiple partial parses) are valuable, and why ambiguity resolution is a core challenge — even humans get confused!

C2 — Abstract Meaning Representation

What are Abstract Meaning Representations (AMR), and why are they harder to parse than dependency trees?

AMR is a semantic formalism that represents the meaning of a sentence as a rooted, directed, acyclic graph — capturing concepts like verb senses (PropBank), named entities, and logical relations beyond simple syntax.

Why harder than dependency trees:

  • AMR produces a graph, not just a tree — reentrant nodes (a word appearing in multiple relations) are allowed.
  • Requires verb sense disambiguation (say-01 vs say-02).
  • Requires NER and coreference resolution.
  • Not anchored 1-to-1 to words — requires abstraction over sentence structure.
  • Less training data available compared to dependency treebanks.

C3 — Beyond Rule-Based Systems

Why did the NLP field move from rule-based approaches to statistical/ML approaches for POS tagging?

Limitations of rule-based systems:

  • Rules are hard to write: language is complex and irregular.
  • Rules are hard to maintain: updating for new domains, languages, or evolving language use is costly.
  • Coverage: rules often fail on edge cases or unseen vocabulary.
  • Portability: rules written for one language rarely transfer to another.

Advantages of ML approaches:

  • Learn patterns automatically from labelled data (treebanks).
  • Generalise to new words via statistics.
  • Can be retrained for new domains or languages with new data.
  • Achieved >98% accuracy — outperforming hand-written rules.

2022 Exam

2022 Q3(a) [4 marks] — POS Preprocessing

Justify why case normalisation and stemming are appropriate preprocessing steps for a patent classification system.

Case normalisation: "Textile" at the start of a sentence and "textile" mid-sentence are the same token. Without case folding the classifier treats them as different features, diluting signal. Case normalisation consolidates them.

Stemming: "textiles" and "textile" both signal Section D. Stemming reduces them to the same stem, preventing the classifier from splitting the same signal across two features.

2022 Q3(c) [4 marks] — HMM Domain Mismatch

An off-the-shelf HMM POS tagger performs poorly on patent text. Explain why and propose two fixes.

Why: Domain mismatch — the tagger was trained on general text (typically news), which does not contain the specialised vocabulary and sentence structures of patent text. Unknown specialised terms will be assigned incorrect tags.

Fix 1: Manually annotate a sample of patent documents and fine-tune the existing tagger on this domain-specific data.

Fix 2: Use a domain-specific dictionary of technical terms with pre-assigned default POS tags as a pre/post-processing step.

2022 Q3(d) [3 marks] — Evaluation Analysis

Three systems are evaluated. System B has the best training performance but worse-than-baseline test performance. System A has a small but statistically significant improvement over the baseline on the test set. What conclusion do you draw?

System B is overfitting. Its high training performance but degraded test performance indicates it has learned patterns specific to the training data that do not generalise.

System A generalises better. Its statistically significant improvement on the test set shows genuine performance gains that transfer to unseen data, making it the preferred system despite smaller training gains.

2022 Q3(e) [4 marks] — BERT + POS Tags

Describe how you would incorporate explicit POS tags into a BERT-based text classifier.

In a bag-of-features model: Each (term, POS) pair becomes a separate sparse feature. "light/NOUN" and "light/ADJ" are distinct features.

In BERT: Introduce a new bank of POS embeddings (one per POS tag), analogous to positional or segment embeddings. Add the POS embedding to each token's input embedding at the input layer. The model then attends to both lexical content and syntactic role jointly through its attention heads.

2023 Q3(d) [3 marks] — HMM POS Tagging Rationale

GPT-3 stated that HMMs are used for POS tagging because of "transition probabilities and the Viterbi algorithm." This is correct but incomplete. What key justification is missing?

The missing justification is: syntax imposes constraints on POS tag sequences.

In many languages, certain tag sequences are much more likely than others — for example, a DET is very likely followed by a NOUN or ADJ, and VERB is unlikely to follow another VERB. By modelling these transition probabilities P(y_i | y_{i-1}), the HMM captures this linguistic prior, producing higher-quality tagging than a model (like Naive Bayes) that ignores preceding tags.

2024 Exam

2024 Q1(a) [4 marks] — Stopwords: BoW vs Transformers

Why should you remove stopwords for bag-of-words models but NOT for transformer models?

Bag-of-words: Word order and syntactic attachment are discarded. Stopwords like "the", "on", "is" provide no content signal — they appear everywhere. Removing them reduces noise and keeps only meaning-bearing words.

Transformers: Explicitly encode word order via positional embeddings. "The cat sat on the mat" and "The mat sat on the cat" produce different contextual representations precisely because the structural words are present. Removing stopwords destroys the relational information the attention mechanism relies on.

2025 Q2(c) [8 marks] — Confusion Matrix Anomalies

A classifier report shows: Training Macro-F1=0.6, Macro-Precision=0.2, Macro-Recall=0.5; Validation accuracy=1.00 with 65 samples vs training set of only 15 samples. Identify as many anomalies as you can.

  5. Training has 2 classes; validation has 3 classes: The class schemas are inconsistent — you cannot evaluate the same classifier on different label sets.
  6. Class distributions are reversed: Training's largest class is Neg (11/15); validation's largest class is Pos (50/65) — the class balance is completely different between splits.

PB2 — BPE Vocabulary Size Trade-offs High Frequency

Explain the effect of BPE vocabulary size being too small (e.g., 100) vs too large (e.g., 100 million).

Too small (100): Only individual characters are representable. Each word is split into many meaningless character tokens. Models cannot learn over such granular units — there is no semantic signal at the character level. Sequences become very long, increasing computation.

Too large (100 million): Every word gets its own token — equivalent to word-level tokenisation. No subword coverage: unseen words are unrepresentable and still need [UNK]. The embedding matrix becomes enormous and impractical to train. Rare words still lack coverage.

Optimal: A vocabulary size (typically 30K–50K) that allows common words as single tokens but splits rare/unknown words into meaningful morphemes.

PB3 — [UNK] in BPE Conceptual

A colleague claims that BPE tokenisers do not need [UNK] tokens because Unicode can represent any character. Is this correct? Explain.

The colleague is wrong. [UNK] is still needed because:

  1. Unicode defines over 140,000 characters — far more than will appear in any training corpus. Characters not seen in training have no learned BPE encoding.
  2. Unicode is continuously extended with new characters (new emoji, scripts, etc.) that postdate training.
  3. Any character absent from the BPE training data cannot be encoded and must fall back to [UNK].

PB6 — Smoothing: Why, What, When Conceptual

(a) Why is smoothing applied to n-gram models? (b) What happens if too much smoothing is applied? (c) Which sequences benefit most?

(a) Why: To avoid zero probabilities due to data sparsity. N-grams unseen in training would get P=0, making any sequence containing them have probability 0 — effectively saying they're impossible, which is incorrect.

(b) Too much smoothing: Dilutes real probabilities. Common, informative n-grams have their probability reduced while rare/spurious ones are inflated — the model generates more nonsensical output.

(c) Sequences that benefit most: Any n-gram with a zero count in the training data — e.g., in Model Y above, P(a|a)=0, P(c|b)=0, P(a|c)=0 would all benefit from smoothing.

PB7 — Sørensen-Dice & Triangle Inequality 2022 Style

Set A = {dog, cat, fish}, Set B = {cat, bird, fish}, Set C = {mouse, rat, cat}. (a) Compute Dice(A,B) and Dice(A,C). (b) Does the triangle inequality hold? (c) What does this imply about Dice as a metric?

(a) A∩B = {cat, fish}, |A∩B|=2; Dice(A,B) = 2×2/(3+3) = 4/6 ≈ 0.667

A∩C = {cat}, |A∩C|=1; Dice(A,C) = 2×1/(3+3) = 2/6 ≈ 0.333

B∩C = {cat}, |B∩C|=1; Dice(B,C) = 2×1/(3+3) = 2/6 ≈ 0.333

(b) Triangle inequality for distance: d(A,B) ≤ d(A,C) + d(C,B). Converting Dice to distance: d = 1 − Dice.

d(A,B) = 0.333; d(A,C) + d(B,C) = 0.667 + 0.667 = 1.334 → 0.333 ≤ 1.334 ✓ holds here.

Note: Dice can violate triangle inequality in other configurations — it is a semi-metric, not a true metric.

(c) Dice is a semi-metric: it satisfies non-negativity, symmetry, and d=0 iff A=B, but the triangle inequality is not always guaranteed.

PB8 — Reverse TF-IDF 2025 Style

Given IDF: A=2, B=3, C=5. A document has TF-IDF vector [6, 9, 0]. Reconstruct the original token counts and determine if word order can be recovered.

TF = TF-IDF / IDF:

  • A: 6/2 = 3 occurrences
  • B: 9/3 = 3 occurrences
  • C: 0/5 = 0 occurrences (absent)

Word order cannot be recovered. TF-IDF is a bag-of-words representation — it only stores counts, not positions. The document "A A A B B B", "A B A B A B", and "B B B A A A" all produce identical TF-IDF vectors.

PB9 — Precision, Recall, F1 Calculation

A binary classifier produces: TP=40, FP=10, FN=8, TN=42. Calculate Precision, Recall, F1, and Accuracy.

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.80 \]
\[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = \frac{40}{48} \approx 0.833 \]
\[ \text{F1} = \frac{2 \times P \times R}{P + R} = \frac{2 \times 0.80 \times 0.833}{0.80 + 0.833} = \frac{1.333}{1.633} \approx 0.816 \]

PB10 — Overfitting vs Generalisation Analysis

System A: Train F1=0.72, Test F1=0.70. System B: Train F1=0.95, Test F1=0.58. System C: Train F1=0.68, Test F1=0.66. Which system would you deploy? Justify.

Deploy System A.

  • System B is severely overfitting — train/test gap of 0.37 means it has memorised training data patterns. Test performance (0.58) is poor and would not work in production.
  • System C generalises well (small train/test gap) but its absolute test performance (0.66) is lower than System A.
  • System A achieves the best test performance (0.70) with good generalisation (train/test gap of only 0.02), indicating robust learning of transferable patterns.

PB11 — Multi-label vs Multi-class Conceptual

A recipe website classifies dishes into: Italian, Asian, Comfort Food, Healthy. Why is this a multi-label problem? Why would treating it as multi-class be problematic?

Multi-label: A single recipe can genuinely belong to multiple categories simultaneously — e.g., "Japanese Ramen" is both Asian and Comfort Food; "Mediterranean Salad" is both Healthy and potentially Italian. Each dish may have any combination of applicable labels.

Why multi-class is wrong: Multi-class forces exactly one label per instance. This creates artificial ambiguity (should "Japanese Ramen" be Asian or Comfort Food?), forces arbitrary choices during annotation, and loses useful label combinations that the model could learn from.

Topic: Distributional Semantics & Word2Vec

PB12 — Outlier Detection with Cosine Similarity 2022 Style

Describe pseudo-code to find the outlier in a word list given word vectors. What is the time complexity in terms of n (number of words) and d (vector dimensions)?

for i = 1 to n:
    c = w_i  (candidate outlier)
    R = V − {c}  (remaining words)
    μ_i = (1/|R|) × Σ v for v in R  (centroid)
    s_i = cosine_similarity(μ_i, c)
output word with smallest s_i

Time complexity:

  • For each of n candidates, compute centroid over n−1 vectors: O(n×d)
  • Compute cosine similarity: O(d)
  • Total: O(n²d) — for small n (e.g., 5-word lists) this is fast

PB13 — Polysemy & Word2Vec 2022 Style

Explain why word2vec produces a single vector for a polysemous word like "bank". What problems does this cause downstream?

Why a single vector: Word2vec trains one embedding per word type regardless of context. "bank" appears in both financial contexts ("bank account", "deposit") and geographic contexts ("river bank", "bank side") — the single vector is pulled towards both senses and ends up as a blend of both.

Downstream problems:

  1. Nearest neighbours are mixed: The k nearest neighbours of "bank" will include both {loan, interest, money} and {river, shore, water}, making word similarity unreliable.
  2. Classification degrades: A text classifier using these embeddings cannot reliably distinguish financial from geographic texts mentioning "bank".
  3. Analogy tasks fail: Word arithmetic (bank − money + river ≈ ?) produces incoherent results.

Note: contextual embeddings (BERT) solve this by producing different vectors for the same word in different contexts.

PB14 — Window Size Effects 2022 Style

What happens to distributional word vectors when the context window k is: (a) too small, (b) too large? Give a concrete example for each.

(a) Too small (e.g., k=1): Vectors only capture syntactic neighbours (the word immediately before/after). Example: "run" and "running" might be close (both often preceded by "I"), but "run" and "sprint" might be far apart despite similar meaning. Semantic similarity is poorly captured.

(b) Too large (e.g., k=100): Context is too broad and noisy — almost every word appears in the context of almost every other word. Example: the context of "dog" in a 100-word window will include hundreds of unrelated words, making the vector non-specific. Vectors converge to topic-level representations rather than word-level meaning.

PB16 — Knowledge in LLMs 2023 Style

How does an LLM like GPT "know" that Tim Cook is Apple's CEO? What are two key limitations of this form of knowledge storage?

How it knows: Knowledge is encoded in the model's parameters during pre-training on large text corpora. The association "Tim Cook" ↔ "Apple CEO" appears many times in training data and is implicitly compressed into the weight matrix — not stored as an explicit fact.

Limitation 1 — Hallucination/Inaccuracy: The model may generate plausible-sounding but incorrect facts. It cannot distinguish between something it has seen frequently (and may be reliably stored) vs something it infers incorrectly from partial patterns.

Limitation 2 — Knowledge staleness: When real-world facts change (Tim Cook is replaced as CEO), the model's parameters cannot be easily updated without full retraining. The model will continue asserting outdated information.
